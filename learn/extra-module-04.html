Extra Course, Module 4: Semiconductors and the Deep Parts Bin

Covers NCVEC 2024-2028 Extra Pool, Subelement E6 (Circuit Components). Six questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray sets a single silicon diode on the bench next to a sliver of circuit board dense with parts smaller than rice. "Same physics in both," he says. "Extra components is where we finally open the crystal itself: what the electrons and holes are doing, why each diode family exists, how logic thinks, what ferrite actually is, why microwave parts look the way they do, and how light became a circuit element. Six drawers. Deep ones."

4.1Inside the Crystal

Semiconductor physics in four moves:

  • Doping creates the personalities. N-type material contains excess free electrons (donor impurities gave them). An acceptor impurity adds holes instead, creating P-type. Current is electrons and holes trading places, and every semiconductor device is an arrangement of N against P.
  • The junction. Reverse-bias a PN diode and it does not conduct because the applied voltage pulls holes and electrons apart, widening the depletion region, an insulating no-man's-land at the junction. Forward bias collapses it, and a silicon NPN transistor shows it is biased on by a base-to-emitter voltage of approximately 0.6 to 0.7 volts, the silicon threshold from the General course now doing diagnostic duty: probe Vbe, read the truth.
  • The bipolar transistor, specified. Its beta is the change in collector current with respect to the change in base current, the current gain as a ratio of changes. Its speed limit has a name: the alpha cutoff frequency, where grounded-base current gain falls to 0.7 of its 1 kHz value.
  • The FET family. A FET's gate presents much higher DC input impedance than a bipolar transistor's base, the insulated-gate construction from the General course paying off as near-zero input current. A depletion-mode FET conducts between source and drain with no gate voltage applied; you bias it to reduce current, the opposite of the enhancement-mode parts in your radio's finals. And the static-electricity vulnerability from General gets its cure: Zener diodes connected between a MOSFET's gate and its source or drain protect the gate from static damage, sacrificial clamps built into nearly every modern MOSFET.

Materials matter at the extremes: gallium arsenide serves in microwave circuits, where silicon runs out of speed. Hold that thought for Section 4.5.

4.2The Diode Family Reunion

At Technician a diode was a one-way valve. At Extra, it is a family where each member evolved for one job:

  • Zener: maintains a constant voltage drop under varying current, run deliberately in reverse breakdown as a voltage reference. The regulator circuits of Module 5 stand on it.
  • Schottky: a metal-semiconductor junction (no P material at all), prized for its lower forward voltage drop than silicon, which makes it the better power-supply rectifier (less voltage lost means less heat), and its speed makes it the standard VHF/UHF mixer and detector diode.
  • Varactor: a diode designed as a voltage-controlled capacitor, the reverse-biased depletion region (Section 4.1's widening no-man's-land) used as a variable-plate spacing. Every synthesized VFO tunes with one.
  • PIN: an intrinsic layer between P and N gives it low junction capacitance, making it the solid-state RF switch, and as a variable attenuator its attenuation is controlled by its forward DC bias current. Your transceiver's T/R switching is PIN diodes doing relay work with no moving parts.
  • Point-contact: the antique that survives as an RF detector, a whisker on a crystal, literally the crystal set's crystal.
  • LED: its forward voltage drop is determined by the semiconductor's band gap, which is also why color and voltage travel together: red LEDs drop less than blue because their photons carry less energy. The Technician "varies by type" answer, explained at last.

One failure mode covers them all: a junction diode subjected to excessive current fails from excessive junction temperature. Diodes do not die of amperes; they die of the heat amperes make.

4.3How Logic Thinks

The digital drawer, from families to fragments:

Families. CMOS keeps its General-course crown for lowest power consumption, and gains an explanation for its famous noise immunity: its input switching threshold sits at about half the supply voltage, the widest possible margin from both rails. BiCMOS hybridizes: the high input impedance of CMOS with the low output impedance of bipolar transistors, each technology contributing its best terminal.

Building blocks:

  • A comparator changes its output state when the input crosses the threshold voltage, and hysteresis (two thresholds, one for each direction) exists to prevent input noise from causing unstable output signals. Without it, a slowly drifting input chatters the output; with it, the decision sticks.
  • Tri-state logic outputs offer 0, 1, and high impedance, the third state letting many devices share one bus by electrically stepping aside.
  • A pull-up or pull-down resistor connects to the positive or negative supply to establish a voltage when an input or output would otherwise float. Floating inputs are noise antennas; the resistor gives them an opinion.
  • Gate symbols (Figure E6-3): NAND, NOR, and the NOT inverter, the AND/OR shapes from your General course wearing the inversion bubble.

The programmable frontier: a field-programmable gate array (FPGA) is configured using a hardware description language (HDL). You do not wire an FPGA; you describe the circuit in text and the chip becomes it, which is how one part on an SDR board can be a filter today and a modem tomorrow.

4.4Iron, Ferrite, and the Singing Crystal

The magnetic drawer, with its physics finally attached:

  • The core property that determines an inductor's inductance is the material's permeability, its appetite for magnetic flux. Insert brass into a coil and inductance decreases, the diamagnetic tuning slug running the effect backward.
  • Push too much flux and the core saturates: inductor saturation is caused by operation at excessive magnetic flux, the core's appetite exceeded, inductance collapsing, distortion blooming.
  • Ferrite versus powdered iron, the choice on every toroid order form: ferrite's high permeability means fewer turns for a given inductance; powdered iron's virtue is the highest temperature stability of its magnetic characteristics. Winding economy versus drift, chosen per job.
  • Solid iron cores waste power in eddy currents, so transformer cores are built of thin laminated layers to interrupt them. The toroid's advantage repeats from the General course: it confines the magnetic field within the core.
  • Ferrite's small-parts career continues: ferrite beads serve as VHF and UHF parasitic suppressors at the terminals of HF amplifier transistors, absorbing the frequencies where the amplifier would rather oscillate. Module 5's parasitic suppression, purchased for pennies.

Then the drawer's oddest resident: piezoelectricity, the characteristic of materials that generate a voltage when stressed and flex when a voltage is applied. Squeeze it, get volts; apply volts, it moves. A quartz crystal is a piezoelectric slab whose mechanical resonance is electrically accessible, and its equivalent circuit is a series RLC in parallel with a shunt capacitance (the electrodes and stray). That equivalent circuit has a spectacular property: the effective Q reaches values no wound inductor approaches, which is why crystals discipline every frequency standard you own, from the DDS reference of your transceiver to the wristwatch timing your FT8 slots.

Exam Anchor (E6D) Permeability determines inductance; brass inserted in a coil decreases it. Saturation: excessive magnetic flux. Ferrite: fewer turns needed; powdered iron: best temperature stability. Laminated cores: reduce eddy current loss. Toroids confine the field. Ferrite beads: VHF/UHF parasitic suppressors on HF amplifier transistors. Piezoelectricity: voltage from stress, flexing from voltage. Quartz crystal equivalent: series RLC paralleled by shunt capacitance.

4.5Built for Gigahertz

RF construction is a fight against Module 3's parasitics, and this drawer holds the weapons.

Materials first. Gallium arsenide earns its microwave role through higher electron mobility: its electrons simply move faster than silicon's. For MMICs pushing higher still, gallium nitride supports the highest frequencies. The exotic crystals exist because speed is a material property.

The MMIC, characterized. The General course named it; the Extra pool specifies it: MMICs offer controlled gain, low noise figure, and constant input and output impedance across their specified frequency range. All of that, in a part costing a few dollars. The supporting numbers: the standard MMIC input and output impedance is 50 ohms (matched to everything by design), a typical low-noise UHF preamplifier noise figure is around 0.5 dB, connections are made with microstrip transmission line etched on the board, and DC power arrives through a resistor and/or RF choke connected to the output lead, riding in on the same pin the RF leaves by.

Packaging is electrical design. The DIP (dual in-line package, two rows of pins on opposite sides) is the classic through-hole part, and it is banished from UHF work for one reason: excessive lead length, every pin a Module 3 parasitic inductor. The cure is surface-mount technology, whose RF advantages stack: smaller circuit area, shorter traces, and less parasitic inductance and capacitance per component. All of these. The rice-grain parts on Ray's board sliver are not miniaturization for its own sake; they are parasitics starved to death.

Exam Anchor (E6E) GaAs: higher electron mobility for UHF and up; GaN: highest-frequency MMICs. MMICs: controlled gain, low noise figure, constant impedances (all); 50-ohm input and output; about 0.5 dB NF for a low-noise UHF preamp; microstrip interconnect; power via resistor/RF choke on the output lead. DIP: through-hole, dual in-line pins; unusable at UHF from excessive lead length. Surface mount: smaller area, shorter traces, lower parasitics (all).

4.6Light as a Circuit Element

The last drawer converts between photons and electrons, both directions.

Light in, electricity out:

  • In a photovoltaic cell, photons are absorbed, and the photovoltaic effect is the conversion of light to electrical energy. The engineering sheet: the most common material is silicon, a fully illuminated cell produces about 0.5 volts open-circuit (the same number your General solar section used), and a cell's efficiency is the relative fraction of light converted to current.
  • Photoconductive material takes the passive route: shine light on it and its resistance decreases. The common photoconductive devices are made of crystalline semiconductor, and every light-activated sensor descends from this effect.

Light as a messenger:

  • An optoisolator (optocoupler) is most commonly an LED and a phototransistor facing each other across a gap. Its purpose in circuits controlling 120 VAC: providing electrical isolation between the control circuit and the circuit being switched. Nothing crosses the gap but photons, so a fault on the mains side cannot reach the microcontroller. The solid-state relay packages this into a drop-in replacement: semiconductors implementing the functions of an electromechanical relay, no coil, no contacts, no arc.
  • An optical shaft encoder detects rotation by interrupting a light source with a patterned wheel, and it is the part under your transceiver's VFO knob. The big knob has not touched a variable capacitor in decades; it has been chopping light beams and reporting the count to the DDS, which is as fitting a place as any to end the components course: even the tuning knob is optoelectronics now.

Exam Anchor (E6F) Photovoltaic: photons absorbed; light converted to electrical energy; silicon; about 0.5 V open-circuit; efficiency is the fraction of light converted. Photoconductive material: resistance decreases in light; made of crystalline semiconductor. Optoisolator: LED plus phototransistor; provides electrical isolation for AC control. Solid-state relay: semiconductor implementation of a relay. Optical shaft encoder: patterned wheel interrupting a light source.

Before you move on

The habit this module installs is the Extra version of the General course's "which one and why": you now pick parts by mechanism.
