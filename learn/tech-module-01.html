Technician Course, Module 1: The Rules of the Road

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T1 (Commission's Rules). Six questions on your exam come from this material.

Meet Ray. He has been licensed for thirty years, his shack smells like coffee and solder, and he has agreed to get you on the air. Ray does not start with electronics. He starts with a question.

"Do you know why the government gives you free spectrum worth billions of dollars, just for passing a test?"

You do not. So Module 1 begins there.

1.1Why Amateur Radio Exists

Ray pulls a battered copy of the FCC rules off the shelf. "Part 97. Everything we do lives in here. And right at the top, the FCC tells you why this service exists at all."

The Amateur Radio Service is not a gift. It is a deal. The FCC sets aside spectrum for hams because the service provides something back:

  • A pool of trained operators, technicians, and electronics experts
  • Voluntary emergency communication when other systems fail
  • Advancement of the radio art through experimentation
  • International goodwill, one contact at a time
  • Self-training in communication and technical skills

"Notice what is not on that list," Ray says. "Money. The word that matters is noncommercial. The moment you get paid to transmit, you have left our service."

That deal explains almost every rule you will learn. Emergency service is why RACES exists, a radio service using amateur stations for civil defense communications. Experimentation is why you can build your own transmitter without anyone certifying it. Training is why you are sitting in Ray's shack right now.

Ray adds one more thing. "When conditions are rough and someone cannot copy your call sign, spell it the way every ham on Earth will understand." That is the phonetic alphabet: Alpha, Bravo, Charlie. Use of the standard ITU phonetic alphabet is encouraged when identifying your station.

Exam Anchor (T1A) The Basis and Purpose of the Amateur Radio Service is in Part 97: advancing the radio art, improving communication and technical skills, expanding the pool of trained operators, and international goodwill. Amateur radio is defined by its noncommercial nature. When an exam answer includes "personal gain" or "business," it is the wrong answer.

1.2Your License and Your Call Sign

A week later the official word arrives exactly the way the FCC delivers it: an email containing a link to download your license. That email is the official notification, and the license itself lives as an entry in the FCC's Universal Licensing System (ULS) database. The database entry is the proof; you are not required to carry a paper copy at all. Best of all, you may transmit as soon as your license grant appears in the database, before any document is printed or downloaded. Ray makes you read your new call sign out loud. "That is you now. Learn what is behind it."

That call sign followed a formula. New Technicians receive a Group D call: a two-letter prefix beginning with K, followed by a district number, followed by three letters (something like KF1ABC). Earn Extra someday and shorter formats open up, which is how vanity calls like K4DIA become possible.

Here is what is behind it. Your license grant is actually two things in one: an operator license (you, the person, and what you are allowed to do) and a primary station license (your station authorization). One license document, both grants.

The FCC currently issues new licenses in three classes: Technician, General, and Amateur Extra. Each one you earn keeps everything from the last and adds privileges.

The practical rules Ray makes you repeat back:

  • Your license term is ten years.
  • If it expires, you have a two-year grace period to renew. During the grace period you may not transmit, but you do not have to retest.
  • The FCC must always have your current mailing address and email in the license database. Fail to keep it current and the FCC can suspend or revoke the license. It is the cheapest rule in ham radio to follow and the dumbest one to break.
  • Want a call sign with meaning? The vanity call sign program lets you request a specific available call. Otherwise you get the next one off the stack, sequentially.

"K4DIA was not an accident," Ray grins.

One more thing while the ink is wet: your Technician license is issued by the FCC, and FCC rules apply anywhere the FCC regulates the Amateur Radio Service, which includes the 50 states and US territories, plus any vessel or craft documented or registered in the United States, including in international waters. Take the radio on vacation to Puerto Rico, or aboard a US-flagged boat in the middle of the Atlantic, and you are still operating under Part 97. When your signal crosses borders, one more boundary applies: international communications are limited to those incidental to the purposes of the amateur service and remarks of a personal character. Chat about the weather and your antenna with a station in Japan all evening; just leave commerce out of it.

Two Part 97 definitions round out the paperwork, because the exam asks for them by the book. A beacon is an amateur station transmitting communications for the purpose of observing propagation or related experimental activities, the automated signal sources you will meet in Module 2 that let hams see which bands are open. A space station is an amateur station located more than 50 kilometers above Earth's surface, which is the legal definition that makes the ISS crew, and every amateur satellite in Module 6, part of our service.

Exam Anchor (T1C, plus T1A definitions) License term: 10 years. Grace period: 2 years, no operating during it. New license classes issued: Technician, General, Amateur Extra. Official notice of a new license: an FCC email with a download link; the license appears in the ULS database, and the database entry is proof; you may transmit as soon as the grant appears in the database. New Technician call format: Group D, two letters starting with K, a number, three letters. You must maintain a valid mailing address and email with the FCC. Vanity calls are requested; sequential calls are assigned automatically. FCC-licensed stations may transmit from US states and territories, US-registered vessels and craft, and international waters aboard them. International communications: incidental to the service plus remarks of a personal character. Beacon: a station for observation of propagation and related experiments. Space station: an amateur station more than 50 km above Earth.

1.3Where You May Transmit, and How Much Power

Ray spreads a band chart across the bench. It looks like a rainbow made by a committee. "This is the map. Your license class decides which roads you can drive on."

As a Technician you hold full privileges on all amateur bands above 50 MHz: 6 meters, 2 meters, 70 centimeters, and up. That "all portions" grant means SSB voice is available to you in some portion of every amateur band above 50 MHz. You also hold limited HF privileges, including CW segments on 80, 40, and 15 meters, and CW, SSB, and data privileges on 10 meters.

The numbers the exam expects you to read off this map:

  • Your 10-meter phone segment is 28.300 to 28.500 MHz, the slice of HF voice that makes worldwide contacts possible from day one when the band cooperates.
  • Two segments are reserved for CW only: 50.0 to 50.1 MHz on 6 meters and 144.0 to 144.1 MHz on 2 meters. Everything else stays out of them.
  • Frequency recognition is tested as pure map-reading: 52.525 MHz is in the 6-meter band (and 146.520, which you will meet again in Module 9, is in the 2-meter band). When a frequency appears on the exam, convert with Module 2's formula or just learn the neighborhoods.
  • FT8, the digital mode you will meet in Module 6, is permitted on all bands where Technicians hold data privileges, which in practice means everywhere you can operate.
  • One reason band charts differ around the world: the ITU divides Earth into three regions, and amateur frequency assignments can vary among them. A US ham afloat near another continent checks which region's chart applies.

Two habits Ray drills in:

Respect the band edges. Your signal is not a dot on the dial, it has width. An SSB signal occupies about 3 kHz, so if you set your dial right at the edge of the band, part of your signal lands outside it. Stay far enough inside the edge that your entire signal fits.

Use the minimum power necessary. The rule is that you use the minimum transmitter power necessary to carry out the desired communication. The legal ceilings come in two tiers: above 30 MHz, where Technicians live most of the time, the maximum is 1500 watts PEP; on your HF segments below 30 MHz, the Technician limit is 200 watts PEP. Either way, the legal ceiling and the right choice are rarely the same number.

"And here is the part that surprises people," Ray says, pointing straight up. "The International Space Station runs an amateur station. Any licensed ham, including a brand-new Technician, is allowed to contact it. Astronauts have licenses too."

Sharing is also part of the map. Hams do not own every band outright. On some bands the amateur service is secondary, which means we must not cause interference to the primary service and we cannot complain about interference from it.

Exam Anchor (T1B) Technicians: all privileges above 50 MHz (6 m, 2 m, 70 cm fully available; SSB in some portion of every band above 50 MHz), limited HF privileges including 10-meter SSB/CW/data (phone: 28.300-28.500 MHz) and CW segments on 80/40/15 meters. CW-only segments: 50.0-50.1 and 144.0-144.1 MHz. 52.525 MHz is in the 6-meter band. FT8 is permitted wherever Technicians hold data privileges. Power limits: 1500 W PEP above 30 MHz; 200 W PEP on Technician HF segments. Frequency assignments vary among the three ITU regions. Keep your full signal inside the band edges. Use the minimum power necessary. Technicians may contact the ISS. On secondary allocations, amateurs must accept and not cause interference to primary users.

1.4What You May Say, and What You May Never Say

Ray hands you the microphone for the first time, then puts his hand over it. "Before you push that button, know the short list of things that end licenses."

Prohibited transmissions, the greatest hits:

  • Music. Never, with one narrow exception for space shuttle era retransmissions you will likely never use.
  • Broadcasting. One-way transmissions meant for the general public are not what this service is for.
  • Encrypted messages that obscure meaning. Codes and ciphers intended to hide the content of a message are prohibited, with narrow exceptions like control commands for satellites.
  • Business communications. You may not transmit for compensation or for your employer's benefit. You may use the radio for your own occasional personal matters, like telling your spouse you are picking up dinner. Selling your own used ham gear over the air occasionally is fine. Running a weekly radio flea market business is not.
  • False or deceptive signals. Never transmit a false distress call. Never deliberately interfere with other stations.
  • Obscenity and indecency. The FCC does not define a word list, and you do not need one. You know.

"None of this is complicated," Ray says. "The service is noncommercial, honest, and open. Every prohibited transmission violates one of those three ideas."

Then he flips the card over, because the exam tests the exceptions as often as the rules:

  • Music's one exception: incidental music included in an authorized retransmission of manned spacecraft communications. NASA's hold music rides along legally; your playlist does not.
  • Encoding's one exception: codes or ciphers may hide a message's meaning only in control commands to space stations or radio control craft. Satellite command links and your RC aircraft are the entire list.
  • Compensation's narrow exception: a teacher may use amateur radio as part of classroom instruction even though teaching is their paid job, because the operating is incidental to their employment rather than communication on the employer's behalf.
  • News's narrow exception: transmitting information bulletins or news is permitted only when it directly relates to the immediate safety of life or protection of property and no other means is available. Amateur radio is not a wire service, except in the moment it must be.
  • Retransmission's exceptions: amateur stations generally may not retransmit other radio services, with narrow carve-outs for space station, propagation, and weather transmissions used for station operations, and the manned-spacecraft case above.
  • One rule with no exception written for it: the FCC prohibits communications with amateurs in any country whose administration has notified the ITU that it objects to such communications. The list is essentially always empty in practice, but the rule exists, and the exam knows it.

Exam Anchor (T1D) Prohibited: music (except incidental music in authorized manned-spacecraft retransmissions), broadcasting to the public, codes or ciphers that obscure meaning (except control commands to space stations and radio control craft), communications for compensation (a teacher's incidental classroom use is permitted), false or deceptive signals, deliberate interference, and communications with countries whose administrations have objected via the ITU. News or bulletins: only for immediate safety of life or protection of property. Retransmission of other services: prohibited with narrow space/propagation/weather exceptions. Permitted: occasional sale of your own amateur equipment, and personal communications that are not on behalf of a business.

1.5Who Is in Control

Ray asks a trick question. "My station, your voice, you talk on my radio. Who is responsible if something goes wrong?"

The answer is the control operator. Every amateur transmission requires one: a licensed operator designated to be responsible for the transmissions from a station. A few consequences follow:

  • The control operator's license class determines the privileges in use. If you (a Technician) are the control operator at Ray's station, the station operates with Technician privileges, even though Ray is an Extra. If Ray sits down as control operator and lets you talk, the station can use his privileges, but he is the one responsible.
  • The FCC presumes the station licensee is the control operator unless documentation says otherwise.
  • When the licensee and the control operator are different people, they are both equally responsible for the station's proper operation. Ray letting you run his station does not transfer his responsibility; it doubles the people holding it.
  • The control point is the location at which the control operator function is performed. With a handheld in your fist, the control point is your fist.

Control comes in three flavors, and the exam loves them:

  • Local control: the operator is physically at the station. You, in the shack, hand on the dial.
  • Remote control: the operator manipulates the station through a control link from somewhere else. Operating your home station from a laptop across town is remote control, and it is legal and increasingly common.
  • Automatic control: no operator is present at the control point. Repeaters are the classic example. They retransmit all day with nobody sitting next to them.

Exam Anchor (T1E) Every transmission needs a control operator. Privileges in use are set by the control operator's license class. The station licensee is presumed to be the control operator; when licensee and control operator differ, both are equally responsible for proper operation. Local control means physically present, remote control means operated through a control link, automatic control means no operator at the control point, and a repeater is the standard example of automatic control.

1.6Saying Who You Are

Your first contact is minutes away. Ray gives you the last rule, the one you will use every single time you operate.

Identify your station with your call sign at least every ten minutes during a contact, and at the end of the contact. English is required for identification when operating phone, and CW identification is always acceptable on any mode.

The corollaries:

  • You may never transmit without identifying, and you may never use a call sign that is not yours.
  • Tactical call signs like "Net Control" or "Checkpoint Two" are fine during events, but they do not replace legal identification. You still give your FCC call sign every ten minutes and at the end.
  • Third-party communications, meaning messages you pass on behalf of an unlicensed person, are allowed domestically. Internationally they are only allowed with countries that have a third-party agreement with the United States. Your unlicensed friend can talk into your microphone while you are the control operator, right there with you.
  • Self-assigned indicators are additions you may attach to your own call, like a new General signing "/AG" or a traveler signing "KL7CC/W3." The acceptable formats are your call sign followed by the indicator, separated by a slant ("stroke" on phone), or on CW by the slant character, in any of the standard spoken forms. The indicator rides after the legal call; it never replaces it.
  • Interference happens. Accidental interference is part of a shared hobby; you apologize and adjust. Willful interference is a serious rules violation, full stop.
  • Repeaters do not pick their frequencies at random. A frequency coordinator, chosen by local amateurs, recommends repeater frequencies to minimize interference between systems. And when someone violates the rules through a repeater, accountability lands where the violation began: on the control operator of the originating station, not the repeater's owner.
  • Club station licenses exist for radio clubs, issued to a club with at least four members, with a designated license trustee holding the paperwork. The club call on your local repeater's ID is one of these.
  • Finally, the openness that makes self-regulation work: your station and its records must be available for FCC inspection at any time upon request by an FCC representative. In thirty years, Ray has never been asked. The rule is why the service can be trusted to run itself.

Ray keys the mic, gives his call, and slides the microphone over to you.

Exam Anchor (T1F) Identify at least every 10 minutes and at the end of a contact. English (or CW) for identification. Tactical calls do not replace your call sign. Self-assigned indicators attach after your call with a slant/stroke and never replace it. Third-party traffic is fine domestically, restricted internationally to agreement countries. Deliberate interference is prohibited. Rule violations through a repeater: the originating station's control operator is accountable. Frequency coordinators recommend repeater frequencies and are selected by local amateur operators. Club station license: at least four members and a trustee. Station and records: available for FCC inspection at any time upon request.
