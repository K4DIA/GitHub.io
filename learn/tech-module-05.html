Technician Course, Module 5: Inside the Radio

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T7 (Practical Circuits). Four questions on your exam come from this material.

A neighbor knocks on Ray's door holding a portable radio. "Every time you talk on yours, mine goes crazy."

Ray does not apologize and he does not panic. He invites the neighbor in, pours coffee, and starts asking questions. By the end of this module you will understand exactly what he checked, in what order, and why he was smiling the whole time.

5.1The Building Blocks of a Radio

First, the vocabulary of the radio itself.

A transceiver is a transmitter and receiver combined in one box, which is what nearly every ham radio sold today is. Inside it, a handful of circuit blocks do the work:

  • An oscillator generates a signal at a specific frequency. It is the radio's heartbeat.
  • The VFO (Variable Frequency Oscillator) is the oscillator you control: it sets the receive and transmit frequency. Spin the big knob and you are steering the VFO.
  • A mixer converts a signal from one frequency to another. Receivers use mixers to shift incoming signals to frequencies that are easier to filter and amplify.
  • Modulation is combining speech (or data) with an RF carrier signal. The microphone by itself makes audio; modulation is how that audio rides the radio wave.
  • The PTT (Push-To-Talk) input switches the transceiver from receive to transmit when grounded. That is all the button does: it grounds a wire.

Two words describe how good a receiver is, and they are not the same thing:

  • Sensitivity is the ability to detect the presence of a signal. A sensitive receiver hears weak stations.
  • Selectivity is the ability to discriminate between multiple signals. A selective receiver hears only the station you want, even with a loud one next door.

Beyond the transceiver, two accessories extend it. An RF power amplifier added to the output increases transmitted power. Some VHF amplifiers have a switch marked SSB or CW-FM, and its purpose is to set the amplifier for proper operation in the selected mode. A transverter converts the RF input and output of a transceiver to another band entirely, which is how hams get on microwave bands using an HF radio as the engine.

5.2When Radios Fight

Back to Ray's neighbor. Interference between a ham station and consumer electronics almost always comes down to a short list of causes, and radio frequency interference in general can be caused by fundamental overload, harmonics, or spurious emissions. All of the above, as the exam likes to say.

Fundamental overload is the big one for neighbor problems: the consumer receiver is simply unable to reject a strong signal that is outside its own band. Nothing is wrong with your transmitter. The cure lives at the affected receiver: block the amateur signal with a filter at the antenna input of the receiver being interfered with.

Ray's checklist when a neighbor reports interference, and the exam agrees with every step:

  1. Make sure your own station is functioning properly, and confirm it does not interfere with your own radio or TV tuned to the same channels.
  2. If something in the neighbor's home is interfering with your station, work with the neighbor to identify the offending device, politely share that FCC rules prohibit devices that cause interference, and keep your own station up to the standards of good amateur practice. All of those together.
  3. For non-fiber cable TV interference, the first step is unglamorous: be sure all the TV feed line coax connectors are installed properly. Loose F-connectors cause most of it.

Interference also flows the other direction. A nearby commercial FM broadcast station tearing up your 2-meter receiver is fixed with a band-reject filter that notches out the broadcast band.

Then there are problems that are genuinely yours:

  • Told you are over-deviating on FM? Your voice is swinging the signal too wide. The fix is free: talk farther away from the microphone.
  • Low RF output from a solid-state radio is commonly caused by high SWR, because the radio protects itself (more on that in the next section).
  • Reports that your FM audio is distorted or unintelligible can mean your transmitter is slightly off frequency, you are speaking too loudly or too close to the mic, or you are simply in a bad location. Any of them.
  • RF feedback, your own transmitted signal getting back into your microphone audio and distorting your voice, is cured by adding a clip-on ferrite choke to the microphone cable.

The theme: diagnose before blaming, and put the fix where the problem actually is.

5.3Feed Line Health and the Dummy Load

Ray hands you a heavy cylinder with one connector and no antenna. "Fifty ohms of pure resistor on a heat sink. Best test instrument in the shack."

That is a dummy load: a 50-ohm non-inductive resistor mounted on a heat sink, and its primary purpose is to prevent transmitting signals over the air while making tests. The radio sees a perfect match, your tests bother nobody, and any problem you observe is in the radio, not the antenna. Testing into the dummy load is step one of splitting every transmit problem in half.

Now the number that rules the antenna system: SWR (standing wave ratio), a measure of how well the load is matched to the transmission line. The scores:

  • 1:1 is a perfect match between antenna system and feed line.
  • 4:1 means an impedance mismatch. Power is being reflected back down the line instead of leaving.

Why care? Most solid-state transmitters reduce output power as SWR climbs, specifically to protect the RF output amplifier transistors. That fold-back is the "high SWR causes low output" answer from the last section, now explained.

Measuring it: an SWR meter or a directional wattmeter in the feed line can determine SWR, and an antenna analyzer is the tool for finding whether an antenna is resonant at the desired frequency without transmitting at all.

Feed line failures come from a rogues' gallery you can memorize:

  • Moisture contamination is the classic coax killer.
  • Ultraviolet light damages the outer jacket, which then lets water enter the cable. That is why the jacket must be UV resistant.
  • Sources of loss in a coax run include water in the connectors, high SWR, and multiple connectors in the line. All of them.
  • Whatever power the feed line loses is converted into heat. It does not radiate, it does not come back, it just warms the cable.
  • Erratic SWR changes point at a loose connection in the antenna or feed line.

Choosing coax: RG-213 has less loss than skinny RG-58 at any given frequency, foam-dielectric cable has less loss per foot than solid-dielectric, air-insulated hardline is the lowest-loss feed line of all, and loss always increases as frequency increases. Fifty ohms is the standard impedance, and the reason coax dominates amateur stations is simple: it is easy to use and requires few special installation considerations.

5.4The Multimeter and the Iron

Two skills separate hams who fix things from hams who ship things back: measuring and soldering.

The multimeter combines the three basic instruments, and each has its own connection rule:

  • A voltmeter measures electric potential, and it connects in parallel with the component, across it, while the circuit runs.
  • An ammeter measures current, and it connects in series, so the current flows through the meter. You have to break the circuit to insert it.
  • An ohmmeter measures resistance by applying a small current and measuring the resulting voltage. Two safety rules come with it: the circuit must be unpowered before you measure resistance, and attempting to measure voltage while the meter is set to resistance can damage the meter.

One classic reading worth knowing: connect an ohmmeter across a large discharged capacitor and you will see the resistance increase with time as the meter's own current charges it. That rising reading is a healthy capacitor saying hello.

Then the iron. Soldering rules the exam checks:

  • Never use acid-core solder for electronics. Acid flux is for plumbing, and it will corrode your circuit from the inside. Rosin-core is the electronics standard.
  • A cold solder joint, made without enough heat, has a characteristic rough or lumpy surface instead of a smooth bright fillet. Cold joints work today and fail on the mountaintop.

Before you move on

You now know the blocks inside a transceiver, the short list of causes behind almost every interference complaint, the health signs of a feed line, and how to hold a multimeter and an iron without hurting anything. Ray's neighbor, by the way, had a twenty-year-old stereo with no front-end filtering: fundamental overload, cured with a ten-dollar filter on its antenna wire, and the two of them are friends now.
