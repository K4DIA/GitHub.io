Technician Course, Module 9: On the Air

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T2 (Operating Procedures). Three questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray slides the microphone across the bench, and this time he does not put his hand over it. "Everything is programmed. The repeater is quiet. Say who you are looking for and who you are."

Your heart rate disagrees with how simple that sounds. This module is the cure for mic fright: know exactly what to say, and saying it gets easy.

9.1Making the Call

The frequencies come first. The national FM simplex calling frequency on 2 meters is 146.520 MHz, the channel where any ham anywhere in the country can listen for a call. Simplex means transmitting and receiving on the same frequency, radio to radio, no repeater in between. The band plans set aside simplex channels so stations within range of each other can talk without tying up a repeater.

A band plan, by the way, is a voluntary guideline for using modes and activities within a band, organizing the space beyond what FCC privileges require. Following it is good citizenship, not law.

Repeaters listen on one frequency and transmit on another, and the repeater offset is the difference between those transmit and receive frequencies. The standards: plus or minus 600 kHz on 2 meters, and plus or minus 5 MHz on 70 centimeters. Your radio applies the offset automatically once programmed, which you did in Module 8.

Now the scripts, and there are only three:

  • Calling a specific station: say their call sign, then identify with yours. "K4DIA, this is KJ4XYZ." Done. No "break break," no drama.
  • Looking for anyone on a repeater: the custom is your call sign followed by "listening." Announcing yourself is enough; someone who wants to chat will answer.
  • Looking for anyone on simplex: call CQ. CQ means calling any station. The procedure: repeat "CQ" a few times, say "this is" and your call sign, then pause and listen. Repeat as needed. And when you hear someone else calling CQ, answer with their call sign followed by your call sign, so they know both who you want and who you are.

9.2Repeater Craft and Fixing What Goes Wrong

Repeaters have their own toolbox, and knowing it separates smooth operators from confused ones.

Access tones first. Many repeaters ignore signals that do not carry a CTCSS tone: a sub-audible tone transmitted along with your voice that opens the repeater's squelch. Wrong tone, no repeater, even with a strong signal. DTMF is the other tone system: the telephone-style signaling that uses two simultaneous audio tones, used for controlling things like IRLP nodes from Module 6.

So when you can hear a repeater but cannot bring it up, the suspect list is exactly three items, and the exam accepts all of them: improper transceiver offset, wrong CTCSS tone, or wrong DCS code. Check all three before blaming the radio.

The reverse function listens on the repeater's input frequency. Why bother? Because if you can hear a station on the input, you can work them simplex and give the repeater back to everyone else.

Some repeaters are part of a linked repeater network, where signals received by one repeater are transmitted by all the repeaters in the network, turning a regional system into one big room. On DMR systems, two more settings from your Module 8 code plug matter on the air: the color code, an access code that must be programmed into your radio to access a specific repeater, and the talkgroup, an identifier DMR uses to organize traffic so that those who want to hear a group are not bothered by other traffic.

Operating problems and their answers:

  • Your FM audio drops out on voice peaks: you are talking too loudly. Back off the mic.
  • Two stations interfere with each other on the same frequency: the stations should negotiate continued use of the frequency. Nobody owns a frequency, and "I was here first" is not a rule.
  • And two Q signals every operator uses whether they know Morse or not: QRM means interference from other stations, and QSY means changing frequency. "QRM here, let's QSY up five" is a complete, universally understood sentence.

Exam Anchor (T2B) CTCSS: sub-audible tone that opens a repeater's squelch. DTMF: two simultaneous audio tones. Talkgroup: DMR identifier organizing traffic; join by programming the group's ID. Audio dropout on peaks: talking too loudly. Shared-frequency interference: negotiate. QRM: interference from stations. QSY: changing frequency. Squelch (from Module 8): mutes audio when no signal is present.

9.3When It Matters: Nets and Public Service

The pager on Ray's belt is a museum piece, but the habit behind it is not. "Someday the cell network will be down, and somebody will hand you a radio and a list of stations. This is how that works."

First, the legal frame: FCC rules always apply. There is no emergency exception that suspends Part 97, no FEMA mode, no special wartime footing. One narrow provision exists: a control operator may operate outside their license privileges only in situations involving the immediate safety of human life or protection of property. That is a break-glass rule, not a loophole.

Two organizations, constantly confused, cleanly separated:

  • ARES (Amateur Radio Emergency Service): licensed amateurs who have voluntarily registered their qualifications and equipment for communications duty in the public service. Join by volunteering.
  • RACES (Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service): an FCC Part 97 amateur radio service for civil defense communications during national emergencies. Operating RACES requires certification of current enrollment by a civil defense organization. Join by being certified.

Ray's memory hook: ARES is volunteers, RACES is certified.

Directed nets run on discipline:

  • The Net Control Station calls the net to order and directs communications between stations checking in. One voice runs the room.
  • Your job as a participant: unless you are reporting an emergency, transmit only when directed by net control.
  • Traffic means the formal messages exchanged by net stations, and formal messages have anatomy: the preamble contains the information needed to track the message, and the check is the number of words or word equivalents in the text portion, so the receiving station can verify nothing fell out along the way.
  • Unusual words in a message get spelled using a standard phonetic alphabet, the same ITU phonetics from Module 1, now doing operational work.

One digital tool belongs in this section: Winlink, the system that relays messages using email addresses based on amateur call signs, moving real email over radio when the internet is gone.

Exam Anchor (T2C) FCC rules always apply; operating outside privileges is allowed only for the immediate safety of human life or protection of property. ARES: volunteers registered for public service duty. RACES: FCC Part 97 service for civil defense, requiring civil defense certification. Net Control calls the net to order and directs communications; participants transmit only when directed unless reporting an emergency. Traffic: formal messages. Preamble: tracking information. Check: word count of the text. Spell unusual words with standard phonetics. Winlink: email relayed via call sign-based addresses.
