Technician Course, Module 7: Antennas and Feed Lines

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T9 (Antennas and Feed Lines). Two questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray holds up two radios. "Fifty-watt mobile into the rubber duck antenna off your handheld. Five-watt handheld into the antenna on my roof. Which one talks farther?"

The roof wins, every time, by a lot. "Money spent on the antenna beats money spent on the radio," he says. "This module is why."

7.1How an Antenna Works

An antenna is a resonant structure, and Module 4's tuned circuit is hiding inside it: the wire's own inductance and capacitance make it resonate, and resonance depends on length.

The master relationship: antenna length and frequency are inversely related. A dipole cut for a lower frequency is longer; shortening a dipole increases its resonant frequency. When your antenna is resonant too low, you trim it. Too high, you add wire. Every antenna adjustment you ever make flows from that one rule.

The workhorse is the half-wave dipole: a wire split in the middle and fed at the center. Its radiation is not equal in all directions. A dipole radiates strongest broadside to the antenna, off the sides of the wire, and weakest off the ends. Point the side of your dipole at the station you want.

Polarization carries over from Module 2, and the antenna is where it comes from: polarization is described by the orientation of the electric field, which follows the orientation of the antenna's elements. Vertical element, vertical wave. That is why mobile and repeater antennas stand up straight and weak-signal VHF beams lie flat.

Sometimes a full-size antenna will not fit, especially on a vehicle. Antenna loading is the fix: electrically lengthening an antenna by inserting inductors in the radiating elements. The coil in the middle of a short mobile whip is doing exactly that, letting a four-foot antenna behave like a longer one. Loading is a compromise, trading efficiency for size, but it is the compromise that makes mobile HF possible at all.

7.2Gain, Beams, and the Antennas You Will Actually Own

Antenna gain is the increase in signal strength in a specified direction compared to a reference antenna. Read that carefully: gain is not free power added to your transmitter. An antenna with gain takes the same energy and concentrates it, like squeezing a balloon: more in the favored direction, less everywhere else.

An antenna that concentrates signals in one direction is a beam antenna, and the classic beam is the Yagi, the TV-antenna-looking array of parallel elements. Of the common antenna types, the Yagi offers the greatest gain. Ray's tape-measure foxhunt antenna from Module 6 is a Yagi built from a hardware store.

The verticals ladder, from weakest to strongest:

  • The handheld's stock rubber duck is the bottom rung: compared to a full-size quarter-wave, its disadvantage is low efficiency. It hears less and talks less, and it is the first thing worth upgrading. Related trap: using a handheld inside a vehicle without an external antenna means your signal is reduced by the shielding effect of the vehicle's metal body.
  • The quarter-wave vertical is the honest standard. On 2 meters it is about 19 inches long, and 19 inches is popular for exactly that reason: it is a resonant quarter wavelength at 146 MHz. Check it against Module 2's math: 300/146 is about 2 meters, a quarter of that is about half a meter, and half a meter is about 19 inches. The formula keeps paying rent.
  • The 5/8-wave whip is the mobile upgrade: compared to a quarter-wave, its advantage is more gain, flattening the radiation pattern toward the horizon where the repeaters live.

7.3The Feed Line's Job

Between radio and antenna runs the feed line, and its whole job is to deliver power without losing or radiating it along the way.

Coaxial cable dominates amateur stations because it is easy to use and requires few special installation considerations: run it along a wall, tape it to a mast, bury it in conduit, and it does not care. The standard impedance of amateur coax is 50 ohms, matching what your transceiver expects.

But coax charges rent, and the rent goes up with frequency: as the frequency of a signal in coax increases, the loss increases. Cable choice therefore matters more at VHF and UHF than at HF:

  • RG-213 has less loss than skinny RG-58 at any given frequency. Thin cable is for short jumpers, not fifty-foot runs.
  • Foam-dielectric coax has less loss per foot than solid-dielectric.
  • Air-insulated hardline is the lowest-loss feed line available, the choice for long runs to serious VHF/UHF antennas.

Connectors are chosen by frequency and sealed against weather:

  • The PL-259 is the standard connector at HF and VHF. It is not waterproof, and neither is anything else: all coax connectors used outdoors, PL-259, BNC, and Type N alike, should be carefully taped for weather protection, because Module 5 already taught you what moisture does to coax.
  • Above 400 MHz, the Type N connector is the right choice, built for UHF and beyond.

SWR returns one more time for its definition: a measure of how well a load is matched to a transmission line. When the match is poor, an antenna tuner (antenna coupler) can help, and its major function is matching the antenna system impedance to the transceiver's 50-ohm output. Be clear about what that means: the tuner makes the radio happy at the radio end. It does not fix the antenna, and any loss in the mismatched line is still being paid. A tuner is a diplomat, not a repairman.

Before you move on

You now know why the roof antenna beat the fifty-watt mobile: resonance, pattern, gain, and a feed line that delivered the power instead of eating it. You also know the upgrade ladder for your own station: replace the rubber duck first, use fat coax for long runs, and seal every outdoor connector like the weather is personal.
