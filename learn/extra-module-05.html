5.1Logic in Motion

Module 4 taught gates at rest; this section sets them running.

Gate behavior, stated as truth tables in prose: an OR gate outputs 1 if any input is 1. A NAND outputs 0 only if all inputs are 1, the AND with its answer inverted. An exclusive NOR outputs 0 if one and only one input is 1, the difference detector inverted. A truth table itself is the list of inputs and corresponding outputs for a digital device, and positive logic means high voltage represents 1, low represents 0.

Circuits with memory and rhythm:

  • The flip-flop is the bistable circuit: two stable states, one bit of memory. Its bonus talent: a flip-flop divides a pulse train's frequency by 2, because it toggles once per input pulse. Chain them and division compounds: four flip-flops divide by 16 (2 to the 4th).
  • A decade counter produces one output pulse for every 10 input pulses, the digit wheel of electronics.
  • The multivibrator family sorts by stability: an astable multivibrator continuously alternates between two states with no external clock, the free-running square-wave source; a monostable switches to its alternate state temporarily, for a set time, then returns, the one-shot timer behind every "key for 3 seconds" function.

Exam Anchor (E7A) OR: 1 if any input is 1. NAND: 0 only when all inputs are 1. XNOR: 0 when exactly one input is 1. Truth table: inputs and their outputs. Positive logic: high = 1. Flip-flop: bistable, divides frequency by 2; four flip-flops divide by 16. Decade counter: one output per 10 inputs. Astable multivibrator: free-runs between states; monostable: temporary state for a set time.

5.2Amplifiers, the Full Taxonomy

The General course's class system, completed:

  • Class A: biased halfway between saturation and cutoff, conducting the full cycle. Cleanest, least efficient.
  • Class AB: each device in a push-pull pair conducts more than 180 but less than 360 degrees, the linearity-efficiency compromise inside nearly every SSB rig.
  • Class C: the General warning restated as consequence: amplifying SSB with Class C yields signal distortion and excessive bandwidth. Constant-envelope modes only.
  • Class D: the newcomer, an amplifier using switching technology for high efficiency. Switching amplifiers win because the device sits at saturation or cutoff most of the time, Module 4's transistor-switch operating points, where the transistor drops nearly no voltage while conducting and passes no current while off, so it barely dissipates. The price: a square-switched output is rich in harmonics, so a switching amplifier's output requires a filter to remove harmonic content.

Stability and topology facts:

  • Unwanted oscillations in an RF power amplifier are prevented by installing parasitic suppressors and/or neutralizing the stage, the General course's neutralization plus Module 4's ferrite beads, deployed together.
  • The grounded-grid tube amplifier's signature is low input impedance, which is why classic HF amplifiers load your exciter comfortably without fancy input matching.
  • The emitter follower (common collector) offers near-unity voltage gain with high input impedance and low output impedance: the impedance transformer of the transistor world, buffering delicate stages from hungry loads.
  • An amplifier's honesty check remains the two-tone test and its intermodulation products, now traced to their circuit cause from Module 8 of the General course: nonlinearity. Linearity is not a virtue amplifiers have; it is a bias point they are held to.

Exam Anchor (E7B) Class A: midway between saturation and cutoff, 100 percent conduction. Class AB push-pull: more than 180, less than 360 degrees per device. Class C on SSB: distortion and excessive bandwidth. Class D: switching for high efficiency; efficiency comes from the device living at saturation or cutoff; output needs a harmonic filter. Oscillation prevention: parasitic suppressors and/or neutralization. Grounded grid: low input impedance. Emitter follower: unity-ish voltage gain, high input and low output impedance.

5.3Filters and Matching Networks

Filters have family names, and each name is a shape:

  • Chebyshev: accepts ripple in the passband in exchange for a sharper cutoff.
  • Elliptical: the sharpest cutoff of all, achieved with notches (nulls) in the stop band. Park a null on the specific frequency you hate.
  • Crystal lattice: Module 4's quartz crystals arranged into the narrow, precise filter that carves SSB and CW bandwidths at IF.
  • The universal high-Q tax: sharp filters ring, smearing fast transitions. CW through too crisp a filter acquires ghost dits. Selectivity and fidelity remain a negotiated settlement.
  • An absorptive filter dissipates unwanted frequencies in a resistive load instead of reflecting them back at the source, kinder to amplifiers than a pure reflective stopband.
  • A receiving filter's steepness gets a number: shape factor, the ratio of a filter's bandwidth at two attenuation levels, describing its ability to reject signals in the adjacent channel. Two more specialists complete the drawer: the cavity filter, a resonant metal chamber whose extreme Q makes it the filter of choice inside a repeater duplexer, separating transmit from receive on one antenna a few hundred kilohertz apart; and in the DSP world, the Hilbert-transform filter, the 90-degree phase-shift network that generates SSB digitally, the phasing method reborn in software inside every SDR from Section 5.6.

Matching networks are filters moonlighting as transformers, and the job description is precise: an impedance-matching circuit transforms a complex impedance to resistive by cancelling the reactive part and transforming the resistive part to the desired value.

  • The Pi-network (shunt C, series L, shunt C) is the classic tuner and tube-amplifier output circuit, matching impedance while low-pass filtering. Its advantage over a simple L-network: the Q of a Pi-network can be varied, letting the designer choose the bandwidth-versus-harmonic-suppression tradeoff, where the L-network's Q is dictated by the impedances being matched.
  • Rearrange the parts and the character flips: a T-network with series capacitors and a shunt inductor is a high-pass filter, the same three components as the Pi, wired into the opposite personality. Topology, not parts, sets the filter type.
  • The Pi-L adds a series inductor after the Pi, buying additional harmonic suppression, the network of choice where cleanliness outranks simplicity.
  • The whole toolbox (L, Pi, T networks, transformers, transmission-line sections) does one job with different tradeoffs in Q, and Module 3 already told you what Q costs: bandwidth.

Exam Anchor (E7C) Chebyshev: passband ripple, sharp cutoff. Elliptical: sharpest cutoff, stop-band notches. Crystal lattice: narrow IF filtering for SSB/CW. High-Q filters ring. Absorptive filter: dissipates rather than reflects unwanted signals. Shape factor: adjacent-channel rejection measure. Cavity filter: repeater duplexers. Hilbert-transform filter: generates SSB in DSP. Matching: cancel the reactance, transform the resistance. Pi-network: two shunt capacitors and a series inductor, matching plus low-pass action; its Q is variable, unlike an L-network. T-network with series capacitors and shunt inductor: high-pass. Pi-L: added output inductor for greater harmonic suppression.

5.4Power Supplies, Regulated

The General course built the supply; the Extra course regulates it.

  • A linear regulator conducts continuously, dropping excess voltage across itself as heat: quiet, simple, inefficient. Its key specification is dropout voltage, the minimum input-to-output difference below which regulation fails.
  • A switching (switchmode) regulator chops instead of drops, and wins on efficiency for exactly the Class D reason from Section 5.2: its pass device lives at saturation or cutoff. High-frequency switching also shrinks the magnetics, the General course's three-pound 30-amp supply explained a second time from the regulator's side. The tax, also familiar: switching noise, the birdies-every-few-kilohertz signature your receiver already knows.
  • Filter architecture matters: a capacitor-input filter yields higher output voltage than a choke-input filter, at the cost of higher peak rectifier currents.
  • The bleeder resistor returns for its final appearance with its full portfolio: discharging the filter capacitors at power-off and improving voltage regulation by providing a minimum load.

Off-grid, the General course's solar rules gain their reasons: the charge controller required for lithium chemistry exists to prevent overcharging, tapering and terminating charge where lead-acid would merely gas and lithium would fail dangerously. The series blocking diode still guards the night, and the controller now manages the day.

Exam Anchor (E7D) Linear regulator: continuous conduction, excess voltage burned as heat; dropout voltage is its minimum input-output differential. Switching regulator: high efficiency from saturation/cutoff operation; high frequency permits small magnetics; generates switching noise. Capacitor-input filter: higher output voltage than choke input. Bleeder: discharges capacitors and stabilizes regulation. Solar charge controller: prevents battery overcharging.

5.5Making and Unmaking Modulation

The modulator and detector circuits behind Module 6 of every course so far:

  • A reactance modulator on an oscillator produces direct FM, pushing the frequency itself; the same circuit on an RF amplifier stage produces phase modulation, the General fact now paired with its sibling.
  • The balanced modulator still emits double-sideband suppressed carrier, and the filter method still carves SSB from it: the General course's transmit chain, now recognized as a specific engineering choice among alternatives.
  • On receive, the product detector recovers SSB and CW by remixing with a local carrier, while AM surrenders to the simplest detector of all: the diode envelope detector, which recovers audio by rectification and filtering of the modulated envelope. Rectify, smooth, listen: the crystal set from Module 4's point-contact diode, formalized.
  • Baseband names what all these circuits move around: the frequency range occupied by the message signal before modulation, your 300-to-3000 Hz voice as it exists before any carrier meets it.
  • FM systems play one more trick: pre-emphasis boosts higher audio frequencies at the transmitter, and de-emphasis at the receiver restores the balance, the pair together riding treble above FM's rising noise floor. It is why FM voice sounds crisp and why music through a flat-response link sounds wrong.

Exam Anchor (E7E) Reactance modulator: on the oscillator, FM; on an amplifier stage, PM. Balanced modulator: DSB suppressed carrier; filter selects the sideband. Product detector: SSB/CW reception. Diode envelope detector: recovers AM by rectification and filtering. Baseband: the message signal's frequency range before modulation. Pre-emphasis at the FM transmitter, de-emphasis at the receiver, jointly flattening the audio while defeating high-frequency noise.

5.6The Radio That Is Software

The General course promised the SDR; this section opens it.

The purest architecture is direct sampling: the RF signal is digitized directly by the analog-to-digital converter, no mixers, no analog IF, the entire band captured as numbers. Two laws govern the numbers:

  • Nyquist: the sample rate must be at least twice the highest frequency in the signal, or aliases appear, false signals folded down from above, the digital cousin of the superheterodyne's image.
  • Bits buy dynamic range: the minimum number of ADC bits is set by the dynamic range required, roughly 6 dB per bit. A band containing S1 whispers beside 40-over locals demands a deep converter.

Once digitized, the workload is arithmetic:

  • Decimation reduces the sample rate by discarding samples once the band of interest is narrowed, and it demands an anti-aliasing filter first, removing content above the new, lower Nyquist limit before the discard.
  • Digital filters split into two clans: FIR filters can achieve perfectly linear phase (no dispersion, clean transitions) at the price of more computation; IIR filters use feedback to do more with less arithmetic at the price of phase behavior. An FIR filter's taps are its coefficients, and more taps buy sharper response, the digital analog of adding filter poles.

Everything the General course attributed to DSP (arbitrary bandwidths, brick-wall shapes, I/Q universality) is these mechanisms. The filter knob on a modern rig is adjusting tap coefficients, and now you know it.

Exam Anchor (E7F) Direct sampling: the ADC digitizes RF directly. Nyquist: sample at no less than twice the highest frequency. ADC bit depth: set by required dynamic range. Decimation: sample-rate reduction by discarding samples, preceded by an anti-aliasing filter. FIR: linear phase available, computation heavy; taps are the coefficients. IIR: computationally efficient via feedback.

5.7The Op-Amp: Gain in a Can

The operational amplifier is Module 4's analog IC, idealized into three properties: very high (ideally infinite) open-loop gain, very high input impedance, and very low output impedance. Infinite gain sounds useless until you cage it with feedback, and then it becomes anything:

  • The inverting amplifier's gain is set entirely by two resistors: gain = feedback resistance divided by input resistance (with the output inverted). Want a gain of 10? Rf ten times Rin. The op-amp's own gain barely matters, which is the entire point: feedback trades raw gain for precision.
  • The non-inverting configuration gives 1 + Rf/Rin, the same bargain without the inversion.
  • An op-amp's frequency response and behavior in circuit are set by the external components, not the chip: the same part is an audio filter, an integrator, or a comparator depending on what surrounds it.
  • The imperfection worth naming: input offset voltage, the small differential input voltage required to zero the output, born of internal imbalances. Precision circuits null it; casual circuits ignore it; op-amp datasheets lead with it.

Your station is quietly full of these: active audio filters, ALC loops, AGC amplifiers, and the analog housekeeping around every ADC from Section 5.6.

Exam Anchor (E7G) Ideal op-amp: infinite gain, very high input impedance, very low output impedance. Inverting gain: Rf/Rin, inverted. Behavior and frequency response: determined by external feedback components. Input offset voltage: the input differential needed to zero the output, caused by internal imbalance.

5.8Heartbeats: Oscillators and Synthesizers

Every oscillator is Module 5 of the General course's feedback loop; the Extra pool names the feedback plumbing:

  • Colpitts: feedback through a capacitive voltage divider.
  • Hartley: feedback from a tapped inductor. (C for capacitor, Colpitts; the mnemonic writes itself.)
  • Pierce: feedback through a quartz crystal, Module 4's piezoelectric resonator holding the frequency with crystal Q.

Modern radios rarely run these free; they discipline them:

  • The phase-locked loop (PLL) closes a loop of phase detector, loop filter, and voltage-controlled oscillator, dragging the VCO into lock with a reference. A PLL performs frequency synthesis and FM demodulation among its talents, one architecture, many jobs.
  • The direct digital synthesizer (DDS) computes its output: a phase accumulator stepping through a lookup table feeding a DAC. Its impurities are characteristic: spurious signals at discrete frequencies, quantization's fingerprints, filtered but never fully erased.

Real oscillators fight the physical world:

  • Thermal drift is tamed with temperature-stable components, notably NP0 (C0G) capacitors, and beyond that with ovenized (OCXO) or temperature-compensated (TCXO) crystal oscillators; the modern summit is the GPS-disciplined oscillator, a local standard steered by atomic clocks in orbit, and the reason your FT8 clock requirement is trivially satisfiable.
  • Microphonics, frequency wobble from mechanical vibration reaching the oscillator, are minimized by mechanically isolating the oscillator circuitry. An oscillator is a Section 4.4 piezoelectric sensor whether you wanted one or not; mounting it softly is the cure.

Exam Anchor (E7H) Colpitts: capacitive divider feedback. Hartley: tapped inductor. Pierce: crystal feedback. PLL: phase detector, loop filter, VCO; performs frequency synthesis and FM demodulation. DDS: phase accumulator plus lookup table; spurs at discrete frequencies. Thermal drift: NP0/C0G capacitors, TCXO/OCXO, GPS discipline. Microphonics: cured by mechanical isolation.

E7A OR: 1 if any input is 1. NAND: 0 only when all inputs are 1. XNOR: 0 when exactly one input is 1. Truth table: inputs and their outputs. Positive logic: high = 1. Flip-flop: bistable, divides frequency by 2; four flip-flops divide by 16. Decade counter: one output per 10 inputs. Astable multivibrator: free-runs between states; monostable: temporary state for a set time.

E7B Class A: midway between saturation and cutoff, 100 percent conduction. Class AB push-pull: more than 180, less than 360 degrees per device. Class C on SSB: distortion and excessive bandwidth. Class D: switching for high efficiency; efficiency comes from the device living at saturation or cutoff; output needs a harmonic filter. Oscillation prevention: parasitic suppressors and/or neutralization. Grounded grid: low input impedance. Emitter follower: unity-ish voltage gain, high input and low output impedance.

E7C Chebyshev: passband ripple, sharp cutoff. Elliptical: sharpest cutoff, stop-band notches. Crystal lattice: narrow IF filtering for SSB/CW. High-Q filters ring. Absorptive filter: dissipates rather than reflects unwanted signals. Shape factor: adjacent-channel rejection measure. Cavity filter: repeater duplexers. Hilbert-transform filter: generates SSB in DSP. Matching: cancel the reactance, transform the resistance. Pi-network: two shunt capacitors and a series inductor, matching plus low-pass action; its Q is variable, unlike an L-network. T-network with series capacitors and shunt inductor: high-pass. Pi-L: added output inductor for greater harmonic suppression.

E7D Linear regulator: continuous conduction, excess voltage burned as heat; dropout voltage is its minimum input-output differential. Switching regulator: high efficiency from saturation/cutoff operation; high frequency permits small magnetics; generates switching noise. Capacitor-input filter: higher output voltage than choke input. Bleeder: discharges capacitors and stabilizes regulation. Solar charge controller: prevents battery overcharging.

E7E Reactance modulator: on the oscillator, FM; on an amplifier stage, PM. Balanced modulator: DSB suppressed carrier; filter selects the sideband. Product detector: SSB/CW reception. Diode envelope detector: recovers AM by rectification and filtering. Baseband: the message signal's frequency range before modulation. Pre-emphasis at the FM transmitter, de-emphasis at the receiver, jointly flattening the audio while defeating high-frequency noise.

E7F Direct sampling: the ADC digitizes RF directly. Nyquist: sample at no less than twice the highest frequency. ADC bit depth: set by required dynamic range. Decimation: sample-rate reduction by discarding samples, preceded by an anti-aliasing filter. FIR: linear phase available, computation heavy; taps are the coefficients. IIR: computationally efficient via feedback.
