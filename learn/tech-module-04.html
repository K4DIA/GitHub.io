Technician Course, Module 4: The Parts Bin

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T6 (Electrical and Electronic Components). Four questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray dumps a coffee can of parts onto the bench: striped little cylinders, tiny tan discs, coils of wire, black plastic bugs with three legs. "Every radio ever built is made of maybe a dozen kinds of parts," he says. "Learn what each one does and no circuit will ever be a mystery box again."

4.1The Passive Parts

Resistors oppose the flow of current. That is the whole job. Need less current through an LED? Put a resistor in front of it. A resistor built to be adjustable is a potentiometer, and you have used one your whole life: the volume control on a radio is a potentiometer varying resistance as you turn it.

Capacitors store energy in an electric field. Physically, a capacitor is two conductive surfaces separated by an insulator. Charge builds on the plates, ready to dump back out quickly.

Inductors store energy in a magnetic field, and they are typically constructed as a coil of wire. Current through the coil builds the field; interrupt the current and the field collapses, pushing back.

If those definitions sound familiar, they should. Farads and henrys from Module 3 were the units. These are the parts.

Switches route current. The naming tells you everything: poles are how many separate circuits the switch controls, throws are how many positions each pole can connect to. A single-pole single-throw (SPST) switch opens or closes one circuit. A single-pole double-throw (SPDT) switch connects one circuit to either of two other circuits. Your master disconnect on a battery box is an SPST doing its one honest job.

Batteries store chemical energy, and the exam sorts them into two piles. Rechargeable: nickel-metal hydride, lithium-ion, and lead-acid, all of them. Not rechargeable: carbon-zinc, the cheap disposable cells. Try to recharge those and you get leakage or worse.

Exam Anchor (T6A) Resistor: opposes current. Potentiometer: adjustable resistance, used as a volume control. Capacitor: conductive surfaces separated by an insulator, stores energy in an electric field. Inductor: coil of wire, stores energy in a magnetic field. SPDT switch: one circuit switched between one of two other circuits. Rechargeable chemistries: NiMH, lithium-ion, lead-acid. Not rechargeable: carbon-zinc.

4.2The Semiconductors

Now the parts that made modern radio possible.

A diode is a one-way valve: it allows current to flow in only one direction. Its two electrodes are the anode and the cathode, and on the physical package the cathode end is marked with a stripe. Push current the forward direction and the diode conducts, but it always keeps a small toll, called the forward voltage drop, and that drop is lower in some diode types than in others. One famous diode turns that forward current directly into light: the LED emits light when forward current flows through it.

A transistor is the part that changed the world. It can consist of three regions of semiconductor material, and it does two jobs:

  • It can act as an electronic switch, turning current on and off with no moving parts.
  • It can amplify, using a small signal to control a larger one. A component that can provide power gain is a transistor, and gain means the output signal (voltage, current, or power) relative to the input signal. All of those definitions are correct at once.

Two families of transistor, two sets of leg names, and the exam checks both:

  • Bipolar junction transistor (BJT): emitter, base, collector.
  • Field-effect transistor (FET): gate, drain, source. FET literally stands for Field Effect Transistor.

Ray's mnemonic: the FET names sound like plumbing (gate, drain, source) because a FET works like a valve, with the gate voltage controlling flow from source to drain.

Exam Anchor (T6B) Diode: current flows one direction only; electrodes are anode and cathode; cathode marked with a stripe; forward voltage drop differs between diode types; an LED emits light from forward current. Transistor: three semiconductor regions, works as an electronic switch, provides power gain. Gain: output relative to input (voltage, current, or power). BJT leads: emitter, base, collector. FET leads: gate, drain, source. FET = Field Effect Transistor.

4.3Reading the Map

Ray unfolds a sheet of paper covered in lines and symbols. "This is a schematic. It is not a photograph of a circuit. It is a map of one."

A schematic is an electrical diagram that uses standard symbols for components. Here is the key idea the exam tests: what a schematic accurately represents is the component connections. It does not show wire lengths and it does not show what parts physically look like. Two schematics of the same circuit can be drawn completely differently and both be correct, as long as the connections match.

The standard symbols you must recognize on sight:

  • Resistor: a zigzag line.
  • Capacitor: two parallel lines with a gap.
  • Inductor: a series of loops or humps.
  • Diode: a triangle pointing into a bar (the bar end is the cathode, matching the stripe).
  • LED: the diode symbol with two small arrows leaving it, light going out.
  • Transistor: a circle with three leads, the emitter carrying an arrow.
  • Battery: alternating long and short parallel lines.
  • Lamp: a circle with a loop or cross inside.
  • Switch: a line pivoting between contacts. Count poles and throws right on the drawing.
  • Variable anything: an arrow drawn through the symbol. Arrow through a resistor is a potentiometer, arrow through an inductor is a variable inductor.
  • Transformer: two inductor symbols facing each other, often with parallel lines between them.
  • Antenna: the branching upward symbol at the end of the RF chain.
  • Ground: descending horizontal lines, wide to narrow.

Website note: the exam includes three figures (T-1, T-2, T-3) that these questions point at. This page will include practice diagrams with each symbol labeled and an unlabeled version for self-testing.

The exam questions on figures are pure symbol recognition: point at a zigzag, answer resistor. Learn the symbols above and the figure questions are free points.

4.4Parts That Do a Job

The last group is functional parts, each defined by what it accomplishes:

  • A rectifier changes alternating current into varying direct current. It is the diode's day job, and it is the heart of every power supply.
  • A transformer changes 120 V AC power to a lower AC voltage. Two coils share a magnetic field; the turns ratio sets the voltage ratio. Rectifier plus transformer plus filtering is how wall power becomes radio power.
  • A regulator is the circuit that controls the amount of voltage from a power supply, holding it steady as loads change.
  • A relay is an electrically controlled switch: a small current energizes a coil, the coil pulls contacts closed, and a big current flows somewhere else. Your finger on the PTT ultimately throws relays.
  • A meter displays an electrical quantity as a numeric value.
  • An LED is the standard visual indicator on nearly every panel.
  • An integrated circuit (IC) combines several semiconductors and other components into one package. An entire receiver can live in a chip the size of a fingernail.
  • Shielded wire is used to prevent coupling of unwanted signals to or from the wire. The braid around the center conductor is a fence for signals.
  • And the one that ties Module 3 and Module 4 together: combine an inductor and a capacitor, in series or in parallel, and you get a resonant circuit, also called a tuned circuit. The two energy tanks trade energy back and forth at one favorite frequency. Resonant circuits are how radios select one signal out of the entire spectrum, and you will meet them again in every license class you ever earn.

Exam Anchor (T6D) Rectifier: changes AC into varying DC. Relay: electrically controlled switch. Regulator: controls power supply voltage. Transformer: converts 120 V AC to lower AC voltage. Meter: displays a value numerically. LED: common visual indicator. Integrated circuit: multiple semiconductors and components in one package. Shielded wire: prevents unwanted signal coupling. Resonant (tuned) circuit: an inductor and capacitor in series or parallel. A resistor's function in a circuit: control the flow of current.

Before you move on

The coffee can of parts is now a vocabulary: passives that resist and store, semiconductors that steer and amplify, symbols that map them, and functional parts defined by their jobs. The resonant circuit, an inductor and capacitor trading energy, is the single most important idea to carry forward, because it is how every radio chooses a frequency.
