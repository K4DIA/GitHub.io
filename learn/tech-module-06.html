6.1FM, SSB, and the Price of Bandwidth

Modulation, from Module 5, is combining your voice with an RF carrier. The different ways of doing it are called modes, and each one trades fidelity, bandwidth, and convenience differently.

FM (frequency modulation) varies the carrier's frequency with your voice. It is the mode of repeaters and mobile radio because it is punchy, noise-resistant, and effortless to tune. FM or PM is what VHF and UHF voice repeaters use, and it is also the modulation used for VHF packet radio. Its costs: an FM voice signal is wide, occupying between 10 and 15 kHz, and FM receivers capture only one signal at a time. When two FM stations transmit at once, the stronger one wins and the weaker vanishes, which is FM's disadvantage compared with SSB.

SSB (single sideband) is a form of amplitude modulation with the carrier and one sideband stripped away, leaving all the transmitter's energy in a single 3 kHz slice of voice. That narrow bandwidth is SSB's advantage over FM, and it is why SSB is the voice mode for long-distance weak-signal work on VHF and UHF. The convention: upper sideband (USB) is used on 10-meter HF, VHF, and UHF SSB. The price of SSB is that it is harder to tune, which is why your radio has an RIT knob.

CW (Morse code) is the bandwidth champion at roughly 150 Hz, the narrowest signal of the common modes. At the other extreme, analog fast-scan TV swallows about 6 MHz, an entire band's worth.

Ray's ladder, narrowest to widest: CW (150 Hz), SSB (3 kHz), FM (10 to 15 kHz), fast-scan TV (6 MHz). Bandwidth is spectrum rent. Pay only for what the message needs.

Exam Anchor (T8A) SSB is a form of amplitude modulation. FM or PM: VHF/UHF repeaters and VHF packet. SSB: long-distance weak-signal VHF/UHF voice, and narrower bandwidth than FM. USB on 10-meter HF, VHF, and UHF. Bandwidths: CW about 150 Hz (narrowest), SSB about 3 kHz, FM voice 10 to 15 kHz, fast-scan TV about 6 MHz. FM disadvantage: only one signal received at a time. SSB disadvantage: harder to tune.

6.2Working the Birds

The timer is down to twenty minutes, so Ray briefs you like a mission.

Amateur satellites are repeaters in the sky. Most are LEO satellites: Low Earth Orbit, circling the planet with a period of around 100 minutes, which means each pass over your head lasts only ten or fifteen. A satellite tracking program tells you when: it shows maps of the satellite's real-time position, the time, azimuth, and elevation of each pass from start to peak to end, and even the apparent frequency including Doppler shift. All of that. What you feed the program is the satellite's Keplerian elements, the numbers describing its orbit.

Doppler shift is the observed change in signal frequency caused by the relative motion between the satellite and your station: frequency reads high as the bird approaches, slides low as it recedes, and you tune along with it.

The frequency plan is named by band pairs. A satellite in U/V mode has its uplink in the 70-centimeter band and its downlink in the 2-meter band. Satellites carry FM, SSB, and CW/data transponders, so the answer to "what modes do amateur satellites use" is all of them. Every satellite also transmits a beacon, a transmission containing status information, and the telemetry it carries describes the health and status of the satellite. Anyone is permitted to receive that telemetry, licensed or not.

Etiquette matters more on a satellite than anywhere else, because the transponder's power is shared among every station using it. Excessive uplink power blocks access by other users. The self-check: your own signal on the downlink should be about as strong as the beacon. Louder than the beacon means you are hogging the bird.

Two last details. Spin fading is the slow flutter caused by the rotation of the satellite and its antennas. And who may be the control operator of a station transmitting through a satellite? Any amateur allowed to transmit on the uplink frequency. Your Technician license is a satellite license.

Exam Anchor (T8B) LEO: Low Earth Orbit, period around 100 minutes. Tracking programs provide maps, pass times/azimuth/elevation, and Doppler-corrected frequencies (all of these), and take Keplerian elements as input. Doppler shift: frequency change from relative motion. U/V mode: 70 cm uplink, 2 m downlink. Satellites use SSB, FM, and CW/data (all). Beacon: transmission containing satellite status; telemetry reports health and status; anyone may receive it. Excessive uplink power blocks other users; correct uplink power makes your downlink about equal to the beacon. Spin fading: rotation of the satellite and its antennas. Control operator: any amateur allowed to transmit on the uplink frequency.

6.3Games Hams Play

The satellite pass succeeds: two contacts in nine minutes with a tape-measure antenna. Ray grins and asks what grid you are in. Time to learn the hobby's recreation department.

A grid locator is a letter-number designator assigned to a geographic location. The planet is diced into grid squares, and exchanging them is standard currency in VHF work and contests.

Contesting is the activity of contacting as many stations as possible during a specified period. Good contest practice is efficiency: send only the minimum information needed for identification and the required exchange. Nobody in a contest wants your weather report.

Radio direction finding is the method used to locate sources of noise interference or jamming, and its sporting form is the hidden transmitter hunt, or foxhunt. The essential tool is a directional antenna, which is exactly why Ray's tape-measure beam exists: cheap, bendable, and perfect for chasing signals through the woods.

Then there is the internet, which hams absorbed rather than resisted:

  • VoIP (Voice Over Internet Protocol) is delivering voice communications over the internet using digital techniques.
  • The IRLP (Internet Radio Linking Project) connects amateur systems like repeaters via the internet, and you access an IRLP node over the air using DTMF tones, the touch-tones from Module 9's radios.
  • EchoLink lets an amateur transmit through a distant repeater without using a radio to initiate the connection, from a computer or phone app. Before using it you must register your call sign and provide proof of license.
  • A station that connects other amateur stations to the internet is called a gateway.

Exam Anchor (T8C) Grid locator: letter-number designator for a geographic location. Contesting: contact as many stations as possible in a set period; send only the minimum needed for ID and exchange. Radio direction finding locates noise or jamming sources; a directional antenna is the tool for hidden transmitter hunts. VoIP: voice over the internet by digital techniques. IRLP: connects repeaters via the internet, accessed with DTMF. EchoLink: transmit through a repeater without initiating by radio; requires call sign registration with proof of license. Gateway: station connecting amateurs to the internet.

6.4The Digital Revolution

Last section of the module, and it is the one that has changed fastest. Ray opens a laptop, plugs it into the radio, and the waterfall display fills with stripes of signal.

"Every stripe is a conversation," he says. "Most of them are too weak for your ears."

FT8 is the star: a digital mode capable of low signal-to-noise operation, decoding signals literally below the noise your ear could use. It lives in the WSJT-X software suite, which also supports Earth-Moon-Earth, meteor scatter, and weak-signal propagation beacons. All of those, from the same free program.

The older workhorses each contribute a concept the exam tests:

  • CW is simply another name for Morse code transmission, the original digital mode.
  • PSK stands for Phase Shift Keying, modulating by shifting the carrier's phase.
  • Packet radio sends data in bundles that include a header with the destination call sign, a checksum permitting error detection, and automatic repeat request in case of error. All of these.
  • That last idea has its own name: ARQ is the error correction method in which the receiving station detects errors and requests a retransmission.
  • APRS (Automatic Packet Reporting System) provides real-time tactical digital communications along with a map showing station locations, and it can carry GPS position data, text messages, and weather data. All of these.

Digital voice gets one technical definition: DMR is a technique that time-multiplexes two digital voice signals on a single 12.5 kHz repeater channel, two conversations sharing one frequency by taking turns thousands of times a second. You will program and use DMR in Modules 8 and 9; here, just own what it is.

Two image and network definitions close it out. NTSC is the analog fast-scan color TV signal standard. And an amateur radio mesh network is a data network using commercial Wi-Fi equipment with modified firmware, hams reflashing off-the-shelf routers into license-band data networks.

Exam Anchor (T8D) FT8: digital mode capable of low signal-to-noise operation. WSJT-X supports EME, meteor scatter, and propagation beacons (all). CW: Morse code. PSK: Phase Shift Keying. Packet includes destination header, checksum for error detection, and ARQ (all). ARQ: receiver detects errors and requests retransmission. APRS: tactical real-time communications with mapped positions; carries GPS position, text, and weather (all). DMR: two voice signals time-multiplexed on one 12.5 kHz channel. NTSC: analog fast-scan color TV. Mesh network: commercial Wi-Fi gear with modified firmware. Digital modes include packet, IEEE 802.11, and FT8 (all).

Before you move on

You can now choose a mode by its bandwidth and purpose, brief a satellite pass like Ray briefed you, speak the language of grids, contests, and foxhunts, and explain the digital modes that dominate the modern bands. The tape-measure antenna that worked the satellite is also the foxhunt weapon, which tells you something about this hobby: the same physics keeps showing up wearing different hats.
