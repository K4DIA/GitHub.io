General Course, Module 8: The HF Station

Covers NCVEC 2023-2027 General Pool, Subelement G4 (Amateur Radio Practices). Five questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray declares it commissioning week. The plan on the whiteboard: tune the station properly, test it honestly, ground it so it neither hums nor bites, then bolt a second version of it into the truck. "Five days," he says, "and at the end you will have the two stations your capstone asks for, and you will understand every knob, meter, and wire in both."

8.1Monday: The Controls That Matter

The HF front panel, decoded one honest sentence at a time:

  • Notch filter: reduces interference from carriers in the receiver passband, surgically removing that one tuner-upper whistling on your frequency.
  • Noise blanker: silences impulse noise (ignition, electric fences) by reducing receiver gain during each noise pulse.
  • Noise reduction: DSP noise cleanup, with a price: turned up too far, received signals become distorted. All cleanup tools smear what they scrub.
  • Receive attenuator: prevents receiver overload from strong signals. Counterintuitive but true: on a loud band, less gain hears more.
  • Reverse sideband (CW): receiving CW on the opposite sideband can move an interfering signal out of your passband while your target stays put. A free escape hatch most operators never find.
  • Dual VFO: transmit on one frequency, listen on another. That is split operation, and it is how every serious DX pileup on Earth is run.

Transmit-side discipline centers on one three-letter guardian: ALC, automatic level control, whose purpose on an amplified station is to prevent excessive drive. On SSB, proper ALC is set by adjusting the transmit audio or microphone gain, and it is the mechanism that prevents Module 6's flat-topping. One critical exception: when transmitting AFSK data, the ALC should be inactive, because its pumping action distorts the carefully leveled tones. Voice wants a chaperone; data wants to be left alone.

For stations running a vacuum tube amplifier, the classic tune-up ritual: adjust TUNE for a pronounced dip in plate current, then adjust LOAD for desired power output without exceeding maximum plate current. And the sequencing rule behind the amplifier's keying line: the transceiver delays RF after keying to give the amplifier's relays time to switch the antenna between bypass and amplifier paths. Hot-switching relays with RF is how amplifiers die young.

Exam Anchor (G4A) Notch filter: removes carriers in the passband. Noise blanker: reduces gain during noise pulses. Excess noise reduction: distortion. Receive attenuator: prevents overload. Reverse CW sideband: dodge interference. Dual VFO: transmit one frequency, listen on another. ALC: prevents excessive drive; set via mic gain on SSB; inactive for AFSK data. Tube amp: TUNE for plate current dip, LOAD for desired output within plate current limits. Keying delay: lets the amplifier switch the antenna first.

8.2Tuesday: The Test Bench

Honest stations get measured, and each instrument has its specialty.

The oscilloscope is the bench king: it contains horizontal and vertical channel amplifiers, and its advantage over any voltmeter is that complex waveforms can be measured, seen, really, in real time. Two amateur jobs it owns: checking the keying waveform of a CW transmitter (hunting the clicks that annoy the band), and displaying the RF envelope of your transmitted signal, fed by the attenuated RF output of the transmitter into the vertical input. That envelope view is where flat-topping shows itself.

The formal test for SSB cleanliness is the two-tone test: two non-harmonically related audio tones through the transmitter, and what it analyzes is linearity. A linear transmitter reproduces the two-tone pattern cleanly; a nonlinear one grows Module 6's intermodulation products, visible on the scope or a spectrum display.

Meters divide by temperament:

  • A voltmeter's high input impedance exists to decrease loading on the circuit being measured. A meter that drags the voltage down while measuring it reports its own interference.
  • Digital multimeter advantage: higher precision. Exact numbers, steady display.
  • Analog meter advantage: adjusting for maximum or minimum. A swinging needle shows a peak or dip faster than flickering digits, which is why tune-and-dip work keeps analog meters alive.

Antenna-side instruments: the directional wattmeter determines SWR from forward and reflected power. The antenna analyzer measures SWR with the antenna and feed line connected, and can also measure the impedance of coaxial cable. Its documented weakness: strong signals from nearby transmitters produce received power that interferes with SWR readings, so analyzing an antenna next to a broadcast station takes patience.

Exam Anchor (G4B) Oscilloscope: horizontal and vertical channel amplifiers; measures complex waveforms; best for CW keying waveforms; RF envelope viewed via attenuated transmitter output to the vertical input. Two-tone test: two non-harmonic audio tones, analyzing linearity. Voltmeter high impedance: less circuit loading. DMM: precision. Analog meter: peaking and nulling adjustments. Directional wattmeter: SWR. Antenna analyzer: connect antenna and feed line; also measures coax impedance; nearby strong signals disturb its readings.

8.3Wednesday: Grounding, Bonding, and the Hum

Wednesday's lesson begins with a complaint: a contact reports hum on your transmitted signal. Ray smiles, because hum has a name and an address: a ground loop in the station's audio connections, multiple ground paths forming a loop that picks up 60 Hz like an antenna. The cure, and the answer to half this section: bond all equipment enclosures together. One fat, short bonding web gives every chassis the same potential, minimizing ground loops and minimizing RF hot spots alike.

The RF side of grounding has teeth. A ground wire is a conductor with length, and at some frequency that length is resonant. A resonant ground connection puts high RF voltages on the enclosures of your equipment, and a ground wire with high impedance at the operating frequency is the classic cause of RF burns from the microphone. The Technician course's flat-strap-versus-round-wire rule was preparing you for this: bonding is about low impedance at RF, not just a path to a rod in the dirt.

Safety grounding gets one hard rule repeated from the Technician course with a new reason attached: metal equipment enclosures must be grounded so hazardous voltages cannot appear on the chassis. And lightning grounds get a rule that surprises people: no soldered joints in lightning protection connections, because a strike's heat will destroy the solder. Clamp, braze, or weld the lightning path.

Interference to consumer gear, the General edition, builds directly on Technician Module 5:

  • An audio device hearing your SSB signal produces distorted speech; hearing your CW, it produces on-and-off humming or clicking. The interference wears the rhythm of the mode causing it, which makes the mode a fingerprint.
  • Cures at the audio device: a bypass capacitor shunts RF harmlessly around audio circuitry, and a ferrite choke on the cable strangles common-mode current, the mechanism Module 4 named.
  • One cause covers interference across a wide range of frequencies: arcing at a poor electrical connection, somewhere a loose screw is broadcasting static across the whole spectrum. Power line hardware, doorbell transformers, and corroded gutters have all played this villain.

Exam Anchor (G4C) Transmitted hum: ground loop; minimize by bonding all enclosures together, which also minimizes RF hot spots. Resonant ground: high RF voltage on enclosures; high-impedance ground wire: RF burns. Grounded enclosures: no hazardous chassis voltage. No soldered joints in lightning grounds: strikes destroy solder. SSB RFI sounds like distorted speech; CW RFI sounds like on-off humming or clicking. Cures: bypass capacitor, ferrite choke on the cable. Wideband interference: arcing at a poor connection.

8.4Thursday: The S Meter, the Processor, and the Band Edge

Thursday is calibration day for your instincts.

The S meter measures received signal strength, and its scale has arithmetic behind it: one S unit is approximately 6 dB, a factor of four in power. So moving a distant receiver from S8 to S9 requires raising your power approximately 4 times, and a signal reading 20 dB over S9 is 100 times more powerful than S9. Sit with that: the difference between your 100 watts and a 1500-watt amplifier is under two S units. Antennas move S meters; amplifiers mostly move electric bills.

The speech processor raises the apparent loudness of your SSB by increasing average power (not peak; PEP stays fixed while the valleys fill in). It buys punch through noise at a cost: misadjusted, it delivers distorted speech, excess intermodulation products, and amplified background noise. All of these. Ray's rule: processing is seasoning, and most operators over-salt.

Then the band edge, where Module 1's map meets Module 6's bandwidth. Your dial shows the carrier frequency, but your SSB signal occupies 3 kHz on one side of it: USB extends upward from the dial reading, LSB extends downward. Work the pool's cases:

  • Dial at 14.347 MHz USB: signal occupies 14.347 to 14.350 MHz.
  • Dial at 7.178 MHz LSB: signal occupies 7.175 to 7.178 MHz.

The operating rules follow by pure geometry: on LSB, keep the dial at least 3 kHz above the segment's lower edge; on USB, at least 3 kHz below the upper edge. The 20-meter phone segment ends at 14.350, which is why 14.347 USB is the last legal dial setting, with zero margin for error. The Technician course said your signal has width; the General course makes you do the subtraction.

Exam Anchor (G4D) S meter: received signal strength; 1 S unit = 6 dB; S8 to S9 = about 4 times the power; 20 dB over S9 = 100 times S9. Speech processor: raises apparent loudness by increasing average power; misadjustment brings distortion, intermod, and noise (all). USB occupies dial to dial + 3 kHz (14.347 = 14.347 to 14.350); LSB occupies dial - 3 kHz to dial (7.178 = 7.175 to 7.178). Stay 3 kHz above a lower edge on LSB, 3 kHz below an upper edge on USB.

8.5Friday: The Truck

Commissioning week ends in the driveway, wiring HF into the vehicle, and every rule from the curriculum's mobile/portable track now gets its General-class depth.

Power first, and the pool is emphatic: the best connection for a 100-watt HF mobile installation is directly to the battery with heavy-gauge, fused wire. Not the alternator, and never the auxiliary power socket, whose wiring is inadequate for the current an HF transceiver draws. This is the Technician course's vehicle-wiring rule with the current turned up: 100 watts of SSB peaks past 20 amps, and accessory-socket wiring at 20 amps is a fuse you did not intend to install.

The antenna is the hard part. The single greatest limitation of HF mobile operation is the efficiency of the electrically short antenna: a 102-inch whip on 40 meters is a small fraction of a quarter wave, and physics collects. The supporting cast from earlier modules assembles here: the loading coil (Technician), the screwdriver's variable base inductance (Module 7), the capacitance hat that electrically lengthens a short antenna from the top, and the corona ball on the whip tip, which exists to reduce RF voltage discharge from the tip while transmitting. High-Q shortened antennas carry one operating consequence you will hear the first time you tune across a band: very limited operating bandwidth, retuning required as you move even within one band.

Noise rides along, and in a modern vehicle the interference sources are the battery charging system, the fuel delivery electronics, and the control computers. All of these. Mobile HF operating is a running negotiation with your own dashboard.

The week closes off-grid, because the mobile/portable station eventually leaves the truck too. Solar power enters the pool with four facts: panel cells are connected in series-parallel to build voltage and current; a single silicon photovoltaic cell produces about 0.5 volts open-circuit in full sun; a series diode between panel and battery prevents the battery from discharging backward through the panel in darkness; and connecting a panel to a LiFePO4 battery requires a charge controller, because lithium chemistry forgives nothing. Your field power box just gained a sun-powered future, with the engineering already specified.

Exam Anchor (G4E) 100-watt mobile power: direct to the battery, heavy gauge, fused; never the auxiliary socket (inadequate wiring). HF mobile's main limit: efficiency of the electrically short antenna; shortened antennas have very limited bandwidth. Capacitance hat: electrically lengthens the antenna. Corona ball: reduces RF discharge from the whip tip. Vehicle noise: charging system, fuel delivery, control computers (all). Solar: cells wired series-parallel; silicon cell about 0.5 V open-circuit; series diode blocks nighttime battery discharge through the panel; LiFePO4 charging requires a charge controller.

Before you move on

Commissioning week delivered both stations from your capstone: the shack, tuned, tested, and bonded quiet; and the truck, wired to the battery with an antenna whose compromises you understand and can name. The two configurations run on identical fundamentals, exactly as the curriculum promised, and you have now proven it with your own hands twice.

Module 9 is the reward: HF operating in full, from your first 20-meter CQ to breaking a DX pileup, plus the CW and digital procedures that make the whole band yours.
