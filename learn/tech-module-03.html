Technician Course, Module 3: Electricity You Can Use

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T5 (Electrical Principles). Four questions on your exam come from this material.

On the bench in Ray's shack sits a military surplus ammo can with a battery inside, a fuse block, a pair of Powerpole connectors, and a master switch. "Field power box," he says. "Before the weekend is over you are going to tell me what size fuse it needs. Not guess. Calculate."

To do that, you need four ideas and one law. That is this entire module.

3.1The Four Ideas

Current is the flow of electrons through a circuit. It is measured in amperes (amps, A). When Ray's radio transmits, electrons are flowing from the battery, through the radio, and back. More flow, more amps.

Voltage is the force that causes electrons to flow, measured in volts (V). Think of it as electrical pressure. A 12-volt battery pushes harder than a 1.5-volt flashlight cell. No voltage, no current.

Resistance is the opposition to that flow, measured in ohms. Everything resists at least a little, and resistance opposes every kind of current: DC, AC, and RF alike.

Power is the rate at which electrical energy is used, measured in watts (W). It is what heats the final transistors and what actually leaves the antenna.

Two supporting facts round out the vocabulary. Current that flows in one direction only is direct current (DC), which is what batteries deliver. Current that reverses, flowing between positive and negative directions, is alternating current (AC), which is what comes out of the wall. The number of complete cycles an alternating current makes per second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz). A radio signal is just alternating current cycling millions of times per second.

Why do electrons flow easily through wire but not through the wire's insulation? Metals are good conductors because they have many free electrons, loosely bound and ready to move. Insulators like glass hold their electrons tightly, so almost nothing flows.

Exam Anchor (T5A) Current: flow of electrons, measured in amperes. Voltage: the force that causes electron flow, measured in volts. Power: the rate at which electrical energy is used, measured in watts. Frequency: cycles per second of an alternating current, measured in hertz. DC flows one direction, AC alternates between positive and negative directions. Metals conduct because of many free electrons; glass is a good insulator. Resistance opposes all current: DC, AC, and RF.

3.2Moving the Decimal Point

Radio numbers span from billionths to billions, so we handle them with metric prefixes. There are only four you need, plus one guest appearance:

  • kilo (k) = one thousand. 1 kilovolt = 1000 volts. 1500 kHz = 1,500,000 Hz.
  • mega (M) = one million. 3.525 MHz = 3525 kHz. 28400 kHz = 28.400 MHz.
  • milli (m) = one one-thousandth. 1.5 amperes = 1500 milliamperes. 500 milliwatts = 0.5 watts.
  • micro (µ) = one one-millionth. 1 microvolt = one one-millionth of a volt.
  • pico (p) = one one-trillionth, seen with capacitors: 1,000,000 picofarads = 1 microfarad.

Ray's method: do not memorize conversions, memorize the ladder. Each step from milli to base to kilo to mega is a factor of 1000, which means the decimal point slides three places per step. Going up the ladder (milli to amps), slide it left. Going down (amps to milli), slide it right.

The second half of this section is the decibel (dB), the way radio people compare power levels. Decibels are logarithmic, which sounds scary and is not, because you only need three anchors:

  • 3 dB = double the power. 5 watts to 10 watts is 3 dB.
  • 10 dB = ten times the power. 20 watts to 200 watts is 10 dB.
  • Negative works the same in reverse: 12 watts down to 3 watts is two halvings, or -6 dB.

Stack them for everything else. 100 times the power? That is 10 dB + 10 dB = 20 dB. Four times? 3 + 3 = 6 dB.

Exam Anchor (T5B) 1.5 A = 1500 mA. 1,500,000 Hz = 1500 kHz. 1 kV = 1000 V. 1 µV = one millionth of a volt. 500 mW = 0.5 W. 3000 mA = 3 A. 3.525 MHz = 3525 kHz. 28400 kHz = 28.400 MHz. 2425 MHz = 2.425 GHz. 1,000,000 pF = 1 µF. Decibels: 3 dB doubles power (5 W to 10 W), 10 dB is ten times (20 W to 200 W), 12 W to 3 W is -6 dB.

3.3The One Law

Ray writes three letters on the whiteboard in a triangle: E on top, I and R on the bottom.

Ohm's Law ties voltage (E), current (I), and resistance (R) together. Cover the one you want, and the triangle shows you the math:

  • E = I x R (voltage = current times resistance)
  • I = E / R (current = voltage divided by resistance)
  • R = E / I (resistance = voltage divided by current)

Then he fires drills at you until the triangle is reflex:

  • 90 volts pushing 3 amps: R = 90 / 3 = 30 ohms.
  • 12 volts across a circuit drawing 1.5 amps: R = 12 / 1.5 = 8 ohms.
  • 120 volts across 80 ohms: I = 120 / 80 = 1.5 amps.
  • 200 volts across 100 ohms: I = 200 / 100 = 2 amps.
  • 0.5 amps through 2 ohms: E = 0.5 x 2 = 1 volt.
  • 2 amps through 10 ohms: E = 2 x 10 = 20 volts.

Every exam version asks at least one of these, and they are free points for anyone who can divide.

Last concept: how components share a circuit. In a series circuit, components are in a single line, so the current is the same through all of them. In a parallel circuit, components sit side by side across the same two points, so the voltage is the same across all of them. Ray's shorthand: series shares current, parallel shares voltage.

Exam Anchor (T5D) E = I x R, I = E / R, R = E / I. Worked values above match the pool problems. Series circuit: same current through all components. Parallel circuit: same voltage across all components.

3.4Power Math and Two Energy Tanks

Back to the ammo can. "The radio manual says 22 amps max draw at 13.8 volts," Ray says. "How much power is that?"

The power formula: P = I x E (power = current times voltage). So 22 x 13.8 is right around 300 watts of DC input. The pool versions you should be able to do cold:

  • 13.8 volts at 10 amps: 138 watts.
  • 12 volts at 2.5 amps: 30 watts.
  • And in reverse: how much current delivers 120 watts at 12 volts? I = P / E = 120 / 12 = 10 amps.

That reverse version is the one you will use constantly, because it converts a radio's power rating into the current its wiring and fuses must carry.

Two more components store energy instead of using it, and the exam wants their vocabulary:

  • Capacitance is the ability to store energy in an electric field, measured in farads. Capacitors are the sprinters: charge fast, dump fast.
  • Inductance is the ability to store energy in a magnetic field, measured in henrys. Inductors are coils of wire, and they resist changes in current.

And one word that will follow you all the way to Extra class: impedance, the opposition to AC current flow, measured in ohms. Your radio expects to see about 50 ohms of it looking into the feed line. For now, just own the definition and the unit.

Housekeeping the exam checks: the correct abbreviations are kHz (lowercase k, capital H, lowercase z) and MHz (capital M, capital H, lowercase z). RF is alternating current at radio frequencies, and its unit of frequency is still the hertz.

Exam Anchor (T5C) P = I x E. 13.8 V at 10 A = 138 W. 12 V at 2.5 A = 30 W. 120 W at 12 V requires 10 A. Capacitance: energy stored in an electric field, unit farad. Inductance: energy stored in a magnetic field, unit henry. Impedance: opposition to AC current flow, unit ohm. Abbreviations: kHz and MHz.
