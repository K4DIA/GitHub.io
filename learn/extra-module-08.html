Extra Course, Module 8: Measurement and the Honest Receiver

Covers NCVEC 2024-2028 Extra Pool, Subelement E4 (Amateur Practices). Five questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray uncovers the corner of the bench you were never allowed to touch: the spectrum analyzer, the vector network analyzer, the good scope. "Extra measurement is about knowing what your instruments cannot tell you as well as what they can. And receiver performance is where amateurs are most often fooled by marketing. Five sections, and at the end of them, nobody sells you anything again."

8.1The Serious Instruments

Each instrument, with its blind spot:

  • The digital oscilloscope's ceiling is its analog-to-digital converter's sampling rate: sample too slowly and Module 5's Nyquist law bites back as aliasing, a false, jittery low-frequency version of the waveform displayed as if real. The scope lies confidently when undersampled.
  • Probe discipline: compensate the probe by displaying a square wave and adjusting until the tops are flat (Fourier in service of calibration: flat tops mean all harmonics arriving equally), and minimize the probe's ground lead length, because Module 3's lead inductance rings measurements at RF.
  • For a linear power supply's ripple, trigger the scope on line: the ripple is locked to the AC mains, so line triggering freezes it.
  • The spectrum analyzer displays amplitude versus frequency, and it is the instrument for viewing spurious signals and intermodulation products from an SSB transmitter. The scope shows the wave; the analyzer shows the sins.
  • A frequency counter reaching above its range uses a prescaler to divide the signal down to countable frequencies.
  • The antenna analyzer's advantage over a simple SWR bridge is that it computes SWR and impedance automatically, and its reach extends to velocity factor, cable length, and the resonant frequency of tuned circuits. All of these. SWR itself is measured by directional wattmeter, antenna analyzer, or vector network analyzer: all of these too.

Exam Anchor (E4A) Digital scope limit: ADC sampling rate; undersampling causes aliasing (false jittery low-frequency waveform). Compensate probes with a square wave until flat-topped; keep probe ground leads short. Line trigger for supply ripple. Spectrum analyzer: amplitude vs. frequency; shows spurs and intermod. Prescaler: divides frequency into the counter's range. Antenna analyzer: automatic SWR and impedance; also velocity factor, cable length, resonance (all). SWR measurement: wattmeter, analyzer, or VNA (all).

8.2Measuring Without Fooling Yourself

Measurement technique is mostly self-defense:

  • A frequency counter's accuracy rests on its time base: the counter is only as honest as the oscillator it counts against, which is why Module 5's GPS-disciplined references matter on the bench.
  • An analog voltmeter's ohms-per-volt rating decodes as loading: full-scale reading times ohms-per-volt equals the meter's input impedance, the General course's loading rule made computable.
  • A directional wattmeter reading 100 watts forward and 25 watts reflected is delivering 75 watts to the load: absorbed power is forward minus reflected.
  • A tuned circuit's Q is measured from the bandwidth of its frequency response, Module 3's f/Q relationship run backward: sweep it, find the half-power points, divide.

The vector network analyzer deserves its own paragraph, because the hundred-dollar NanoVNA has made this a mainstream amateur instrument. Its language is S parameters, whose subscripts name the ports measured: S11 is the input-port reflection (return loss, the VSWR-equivalent), S21 is forward gain through the device. A two-port VNA therefore measures a filter's frequency response end to end, along with input and output impedance and reflection coefficient. All of these. Its honesty ritual is calibration against three standards: a short, an open, and a 50-ohm load, teaching the instrument what perfection looks like before it judges anything else.

The formal transmitter linearity test carries over from General with its instrumentation specified: two non-harmonically related audio tones in, spectrum analyzer on the output, intermodulation products read directly.

Exam Anchor (E4B) Frequency counter accuracy: the time base. Voltmeter input impedance: full scale times ohms-per-volt. 100 W forward, 25 W reflected: 75 W absorbed. Q: from the frequency-response bandwidth. S-parameter subscripts: the ports; S11 input return loss, S21 forward gain. Two-port VNA: filter response, impedances, reflection coefficient (all). VNA calibration: short, open, 50 ohms. IMD test: two non-harmonic audio tones plus a spectrum analyzer.

8.3The Receiver's Noise Ledger

Receiver performance begins at the bottom, with noise:

  • The universe sets the floor: -174 dBm is the thermal noise in a 1 Hz bandwidth at room temperature, the quietest any input can theoretically be. A receiver's noise figure is how far, in dB, its own added noise sits above that theoretical minimum.
  • Bandwidth buys noise: widen from 50 Hz to 1000 Hz and the noise floor rises 13 dB (10 log of 20). This is the arithmetic underneath three courses of "match the bandwidth to the mode." MDS, the minimum discernible signal, names the weakest signal detectable above that floor.
  • The low-band paradox, resolved: input attenuation on 160 or 80 meters barely hurts signal-to-noise, because atmospheric noise exceeds receiver noise even after attenuation. The band's own roar drowns the receiver's whisper; attenuate both and the ratio survives. This is also Module 7's receiving-antenna license, restated from the receiver's side.

Then the modern failure modes, all children of the strong-signal environment:

  • Reciprocal mixing: the local oscillator's phase noise mixes with strong adjacent signals, painting noise onto your quiet frequency. In an SDR, the same disease lives in the master clock oscillator's phase noise. Oscillator purity is receiver performance.
  • The architectural defenses: a high IF makes image rejection easier for the front-end filtering (the image sits farther away), a narrow roofing filter improves blocking dynamic range by discarding strong near-frequency signals before they reach vulnerable stages, and the front-end preselector remains the cure for strong out-of-band signals. IF shift handles the tactical case, sliding the passband away from an adjacent interferer.
  • One FM-specific behavior gets named: capture effect, the suppression of the weaker of two same-frequency FM signals by the stronger, the Technician course's "FM winner takes all" with its proper title.
  • And the SDR's hard ceiling: overload occurs when input signals exceed the ADC's reference voltage, Module 5's converter running out of numbers.

Exam Anchor (E4C) -174 dBm: thermal noise, 1 Hz, room temperature. Noise figure: dB above the theoretical minimum. 50 to 1000 Hz bandwidth: +13 dB noise floor. MDS: minimum discernible signal. Low-band attenuation is nearly free: atmospheric noise dominates. Reciprocal mixing: LO (or SDR master clock) phase noise mixing with strong adjacent signals. High IF: easier image rejection. Roofing filter: better blocking dynamic range. Preselector: rejects out-of-band signals. IF shift: dodges adjacent interference. Capture effect: stronger FM signal suppresses the weaker. SDR overload: signals exceeding the ADC reference voltage.

8.4Dynamic Range and the Numbers That Matter

The specification sheet, decoded:

  • Blocking dynamic range: the difference in dB between the noise floor and the signal level causing 1 dB of gain compression. It is the receiver's headroom, and poor dynamic range shows up as cross-modulation and desensitization from strong adjacent signals: the loud neighbor audible everywhere, the band mysteriously deaf.
  • Desensitization itself: reduced sensitivity caused by a strong signal near the received frequency, and its field cure is Section 8.3's logic inverted: insert attenuation before the first RF stage, sacrificing gain to restore linearity.
  • Intermodulation keeps its cause (nonlinear circuits) and gains its ugliest habitat: two nearby repeaters interfering because their output signals mix in the final amplifier of one or both transmitters. The cure is a properly terminated circulator at the transmitter output, a ferrite one-way valve (Module 4's ferrite physics, promoted) that lets power out but not in. And the reason odd-order products obsess receiver designers stands from General: odd-order products of in-band signals land in-band.
  • The benchmark number: a third-order intercept of 40 dBm means a pair of 40 dBm input signals would theoretically produce third-order products equal in amplitude to the inputs themselves. The intercept is a mathematical extrapolation, never an operating condition, but higher means more linear, and it is the one number on the specification sheet that predicts crowded-band behavior.

The engineering payoff is the link budget arithmetic, General Module 6's concept now computed in dBm: received level = transmit power + gains - losses. The pool's pair: 10 W (+40 dBm) + 6 dBi + 3 dBi - 100 dB path = -51 dBm received. And link margin = received level minus (MDS + required SNR): +40 +10 -3 -136 = -89 dBm received, against -103 dBm MDS plus 6 dB required = -97 dBm needed, margin +8 dB. One last unit check the pool loves: -100 dBm is 0.1 picowatt. Ten orders of magnitude below your transmit watt, and routinely copied: that number is the whole miracle of radio, stated in dB.

Exam Anchor (E4D) Blocking dynamic range: noise floor to 1 dB compression. Poor dynamic range: cross-modulation and desense. Desensitization: strong nearby signal; cure: attenuation ahead of the first RF stage. Repeater intermod: outputs mixing in a final amplifier; cure: terminated circulator. Odd-order products fall in-band. Third-order intercept 40 dBm: theoretical equal-amplitude intermod at 40 dBm inputs. Link math: +40 dBm +6 +3 -100 = -51 dBm; the margin example computes to +8 dB. -100 dBm = 0.1 picowatt.

8.5The Noise Around You

The final measurement discipline is the noisiest one: everything radiating in and around your own station.

Identification by fingerprint:

  • Switch-mode power supplies: carriers at regular intervals across a wide frequency range, the harmonics of the switching frequency marching up the band.
  • Computer network equipment: unstable modulated or unmodulated signals at specific frequencies.
  • Arcing in its loud AC form: intermittent roaring or buzzing from arcing thermostats, defective doorbell transformers, or failing illuminated signs. All of these.
  • The strangest one: corroded metal connections mixing and re-radiating local AM broadcast signals, a rusty downspout as an unlicensed mixer, generating Section 8.4's intermodulation with no electronics at all.

Suppression by mechanism:

  • Vehicle charging-system noise: ferrite chokes on the charging system leads. AC motor noise: a brute-force AC line filter in the power leads. Each cure blocks the conducted path.
  • Common-mode current, the recurring villain since the Technician course, gets its formal definition: current flowing equally on all conductors of a cable (or on a shield), and it is exactly what makes shielded cables radiate or receive interference despite the shield. The shield only works when the noise is differential; common-mode rides the outside.
  • The receiver-side tools carry their known limits: the noise blanker removes impulse noise but can make strong signals appear distorted and spurious; digital noise reduction helps against most noise types; and the automatic notch filter must be off for CW, because it will efficiently remove the CW signal itself, which is, after all, a carrier.

The panel that ties three courses together: lightning protection concludes with the single point ground panel, the grounded entry plate from your General station now named and specified. Its purpose: ensuring all lightning protectors activate at the same time, no potential differences between protected lines for a surge to exploit. And the station's AC surge protector belongs on that same panel, unifying every conductor's protection at one point. The panel is the physical answer to a question all three safety modules have been building toward: where does everything meet? Here.

Exam Anchor (E4E) Switch-mode supplies: regular carriers across a wide spectrum. Network gear: unstable signals at specific frequencies. Roaring/buzzing line noise: arcing thermostats, doorbells, signs (all). Corroded connections re-radiate mixed broadcast signals. Vehicle charging noise: ferrite chokes on the leads. AC motors: brute-force line filter. Common-mode current: equal on all conductors; makes shielded cable radiate or receive. Noise blanker: distorts strong signals. Digital NR: broadly effective. Auto-notch: removes CW signals; disable for CW. Single point ground panel: all protectors fire together; the AC surge protector mounts on it.

E4E Switch-mode supplies: regular carriers across a wide spectrum. Network gear: unstable signals at specific frequencies. Roaring/buzzing line noise: arcing thermostats, doorbells, signs (all). Corroded connections re-radiate mixed broadcast signals. Vehicle charging noise: ferrite chokes on the leads. AC motors: brute-force line filter. Common-mode current: equal on all conductors; makes shielded cable radiate or receive. Noise blanker: distorts strong signals. Digital NR: broadly effective. Auto-notch: removes CW signals; disable for CW. Single point ground panel: all protectors fire together; the AC surge protector mounts on it.
