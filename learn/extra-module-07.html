Extra Course, Module 7: Antennas as Engineering

Covers NCVEC 2024-2028 Extra Pool, Subelement E9 (Antennas and Transmission Lines). Eight questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray rolls a whiteboard out to the antenna field. "Technician gave you antennas as facts, General gave you antennas as math. Extra gives you antennas as engineering: efficiency budgets, patterns you can predict before you build, matching you can design instead of buy, and the one chart that makes transmission lines visible. Eight sections. This is the module your capstone project lives in."

7.1The Antenna's Balance Sheet

Every antenna keeps books, and radiation resistance is the honest column: the equivalent resistance that would dissipate the same power the antenna actually radiates. It is not a real resistor; it is radiation itself, wearing Ohm's Law. The feed point resistance you measure is the sum of radiation resistance plus loss resistance, and the ratio is everything:

Efficiency = radiation resistance / (radiation resistance + loss resistance)

That one fraction explains three courses of advice. Electrically short antennas (the mobile whips of General Module 8) have very low radiation resistance, so even small loss resistances eat a huge share. Ground-mounted verticals lose power in the dirt, so their efficiency is improved by more and better radials, shrinking the loss term.

The reference standards, formalized: an isotropic radiator is the theoretical antenna radiating equally in all directions, the zero point of dBi. The 2.15 dB offset to dBd carries over from General. From gain and losses comes effective radiated power: transmitter power adjusted by every system gain and loss, referenced to a dipole (ERP) or to isotropic (EIRP). The method is pure decibel bookkeeping: sum the dB, convert. A repeater running 150 watts through 2 dB of feed line loss and 2.2 dB of duplexer loss into a 7 dBd antenna nets +2.8 dB, for an ERP of about 286 watts. The FCC bought this arithmetic for the 630- and 2200-meter limits in Module 1; now you can audit it.

Directivity's measure: beamwidth is the angle between the half-power (3 dB) points of the main lobe, and it trades directly against gain: squeeze the pattern narrower and the gain rises, the balloon metaphor from the Technician course now wearing numbers.

Exam Anchor (E9A) Radiation resistance: the resistance equivalent to the radiated power. Feed point resistance = radiation + loss resistance; efficiency is their ratio. Short antennas: very low radiation resistance. Vertical efficiency: improved with a better radial system. Isotropic radiator: equal radiation in all directions, the dBi reference. ERP/EIRP: power adjusted by all gains and losses (150 W, -2, -2.2, +7 dBd = about 286 W ERP). Beamwidth: angle between half-power points; higher gain, narrower beamwidth.

7.2Patterns You Can Predict

Antenna software turned pattern prediction from art to engineering, and the pool wants the method: antenna modeling by the method of moments, in which the wire is divided into segments and the current in each segment is computed. More segments, more accuracy, longer computation: the modeler's eternal dial. What the model produces is the far field pattern, the region where the pattern's shape no longer depends on distance from the antenna, which is the pattern that matters to anyone you contact.

Reading the plots is its own literacy. An azimuth or elevation pattern (like the pool's Figure E9-1) yields the numbers by inspection: gain from the main lobe's reach, front-to-back ratio by comparing the main lobe to the 180-degree point, beamwidth between the 3 dB points. The pool's figure questions are pure plot-reading, and practicing on your own model's output makes them free points.

The modeling habit pays off immediately in your capstone (E-S2 requires modeling, building, and comparing), and the comparison step is where the education lives: the model assumed perfect ground and exact dimensions; the antenna hangs in a real yard. The gap between them is a list of everything this module teaches.

7.3Wires, Phasing, and the Ground Beneath

The wire-antenna drawer, Extra depth:

  • The folded dipole wraps the dipole back on itself, multiplying feed impedance by four: about 300 ohms, which is why 300-ohm twin-lead and folded dipoles grew up together.
  • The terminated rhombic is the wire antenna scaled to ambition: unidirectional, extremely wideband, requiring acres and a terminating resistor that burns half the power to buy its clean unidirectional pattern.
  • Plain long wires develop gain as they grow, and their lobes align closer to the wire's axis as length increases, the pattern folding forward along the wire.

Phased arrays are Module 3's phase mathematics radiating: two elements, one geometry lever (spacing) and one electrical lever (feed phase):

  • Quarter-wave spacing, fed 90 degrees apart: a cardioid, the heart-shaped pattern with one deep null, beloved of direction finders and low-band receivers.
  • Half-wave spacing, fed in phase: broadside figure-eight, gain perpendicular to the pair.
  • Half-wave spacing, fed 180 degrees apart: end-fire figure-eight, gain along the line of the pair.

Same two verticals, three different antennas, chosen with a feed line.

Ground finishes the section as an active element: the elevation lobes of every horizontal antenna are interference patterns between direct and ground-reflected waves, which is how height sets takeoff angle. Ground quality scales the effect: over saltwater, with its high conductivity, a vertical's low-angle radiation is dramatically enhanced, the reason DXpeditions camp on beaches, and sloping terrain tilts the whole pattern with it.

Exam Anchor (E9C) Folded dipole: about 300 ohms. Terminated rhombic: unidirectional, wideband, large, and terminated in a resistor. Long wires: lobes align toward the wire as length grows. Two-element phasing: 90 degrees at 1/4-wave spacing = cardioid; in phase at 1/2 wave = broadside figure-eight; 180 degrees at 1/2 wave = end-fire figure-eight. Ground reflections form the elevation lobes; saltwater's conductivity enhances low-angle vertical radiation.

7.4Dishes and the Physics of Short

Two extremes of the antenna world share this section.

The parabolic dish is gain by geometry: its aperture captures energy proportional to area measured in wavelengths, so doubling the frequency adds 6 dB of gain from the same dish. The same three-foot dish that is modest at 2.4 GHz is formidable at 10 GHz, which is the economic engine of amateur microwave work.

The electrically short antenna is the opposite problem, and Extra finally supplies the full physics behind the General course's mobile whip:

  • A short antenna's feed point shows capacitive reactance (it is an unfinished antenna, and unfinished antennas look like capacitors), so loading inductance exists to cancel that capacitive reactance and bring the feed point to resonance. The loading coil is a conjugate match, not a lengthener in any physical sense.
  • Coil placement is an efficiency decision: moving the coil from the base toward the center of the whip improves the current distribution along the radiator and with it the radiation efficiency. Better still is top loading (the capacitance hat from General Module 8): finishing the antenna capacitively at the tip keeps current high along the whole radiator, the most efficient fix of all.
  • The coil itself must be high Q to keep its loss resistance small, because Section 7.1's balance sheet is brutal here: with radiation resistance of a few ohms, every ohm of coil loss is a large slice of your power. And Q keeps its Module 3 bill: the higher the Q, the narrower the bandwidth, the retune-across-the-band behavior your mobile antenna has always had, now fully accounted for.

Exam Anchor (E9D) Parabolic dish: +6 dB each time frequency doubles. Short antennas present capacitive reactance; loading inductance cancels it. Center loading beats base loading on radiation efficiency; top loading (capacitance hat) is better still. Loading coils must be high Q to limit loss against the antenna's very low radiation resistance; high Q narrows operating bandwidth.

7.5Matching, Designed

The General course bought matching devices; this section designs them.

  • The gamma match (from General, now with its missing part explained): the matching rod paralleling the driven element is inductive, and the series capacitor exists to cancel that inductive reactance. The whole assembly taps the element at the 50-ohm point without breaking it, hence no insulation from the boom. Its wire-antenna cousin, the delta match, fans the feed line out to attach at two points spread along an unbroken element, the spread choosing the impedance the same way the gamma's tap point does. And the hairpin (beta) match from your General course keeps its place here: a shorted stub across the feed point supplying the inductance that a slightly shortened driven element needs.
  • Stub matching wields Section 7.6's transmission-line reactances deliberately: a shorted or open stub of the right length places a computed reactance in parallel with the feed point, cancelling what the antenna presents. A stub is a capacitor or inductor made of coax, free from a scrap box. The same line-length arithmetic serves Section 7.3's arrays as phasing lines: measured lengths of feed line delivering the 90- or 180-degree feed relationships that steer the pattern.
  • The quarter-wave transformer is matching by geometry: a quarter wavelength of line whose impedance is the geometric mean of the two impedances to be matched, Z = the square root of Z1 times Z2. Match a 100-ohm antenna to 50-ohm coax through a quarter wave of 70-ohm line (75-ohm cable, close enough, is the classic trick); match 50 to 200 through 100-ohm line. Module 3's transformer arithmetic, rebuilt from cable.
  • The Wilkinson divider solves the phased-array plumbing problem: it divides power equally between two loads while maintaining match and providing isolation between the output ports, so the two antennas of Section 7.3's array each get their share without talking to each other through the splitter.

Exam Anchor (E9E) Gamma match series capacitor: cancels the matching section's inductive reactance. Delta match: feed line fanned to two attachment points on an unbroken element. Hairpin/beta match: shorted stub supplying inductance at the feed point. Phasing lines: measured line lengths setting element feed phase. Stubs: shorted or open line sections supplying cancelling reactance. Quarter-wave transformer: line impedance equals the square root of the product of the two impedances (50 to 100 via about 70-75 ohms; 50 to 200 via 100 ohms). Wilkinson divider: equal power split with isolation between output ports.

7.6The Transmission Line's Secret Life

A transmission line does more than carry power; cut to the right lengths, it becomes components.

Velocity factor first: the ratio of the wave's velocity in the line to its velocity in free space, set by the dielectric (Module 2's index of refraction, indoors). Solid polyethylene coax runs about 0.66; foam, around 0.8. The consequence: a line's electrical length exceeds its physical length, so a quarter wavelength of solid-dielectric coax at 14.1 MHz is not 17.5 feet of cable but 17.5 x 0.66, about 11.5 feet. Every stub and transformer from Section 7.5 is cut by this arithmetic.

Now the secret life. A line terminated in a short or open reflects everything, and at the input, the reflection reads as pure reactance that depends on length:

  • Shorted quarter wave: looks open (very high impedance). Open quarter wave: looks shorted. The quarter wave inverts.
  • Shorted eighth wave: inductive reactance. Open eighth wave: capacitive reactance. The stub components of Section 7.5, specified.
  • Half wave (shorted or open): repeats its far-end condition exactly. The half wave is a mirror, which is also why a half wave of any line reproduces the antenna's impedance at the shack end, a measurement trick worth remembering.

The medium itself completes the ledger from three courses of feed line talk: open-wire line carries the lowest loss of the practical lines, coax buys convenience with loss, and at microwave the transmission line dissolves into the circuit board itself as microstrip: a precisely dimensioned trace over a ground plane, the controlled-impedance line that connects Module 4's MMICs.

Exam Anchor (E9F) Velocity factor: line velocity over free-space velocity, set by the dielectric; solid polyethylene about 0.66. Physical length = electrical length times VF (quarter wave at 14.1 MHz in 0.66 coax: about 11.5 feet). Shorted 1/4 wave: open at input; open 1/4 wave: short. Shorted 1/8 wave: inductive; open 1/8 wave: capacitive. Half wave: repeats the termination. Open-wire line: lowest loss. Microstrip: controlled-impedance PCB transmission line for microwave.

7.7The Smith Chart

One page of curves makes Section 7.6 visible: the Smith chart, whose coordinate system consists of resistance circles and reactance arcs. Every possible impedance is one point; the chart's uses are impedance, SWR, and transmission line calculations performed as geometry.

The working rules:

  • The chart is normalized, customarily to 50 ohms, so the center point (1.0) is a perfect 50-ohm match. Divide your impedance by 50, plot, work, multiply back.
  • Moving along a transmission line rotates the impedance point around the chart, distance marked on the wavelength scales around the perimeter. A half wavelength is one full lap, which is Section 7.6's half-wave mirror drawn as a circle.
  • Points of equal mismatch trace constant-SWR circles centered on the chart's center: the third family of circles, drawn in by the user. A lossless line moves your impedance around its SWR circle; a matching network's job is spiraling that point into the center.

Your NanoVNA has been drawing this chart since Module 3's tie-in; now the display is readable. The gamma capacitor, the stub, the quarter-wave transformer: each Section 7.5 device is one specific move on this chart, and designing a match becomes navigation.

7.8Antennas That Only Listen

The receiving-antenna drawer, where efficiency stops mattering and directivity is everything. The license for that trade: on the low bands, atmospheric noise towers over receiver noise, so an inefficient antenna loses signal and noise together, costing nothing in signal-to-noise while its pattern can be optimized purely for rejection.

  • The Beverage (introduced at General) gets its design rule: for good directivity it must be one or more wavelengths long, the terminated long wire earning its low-band listening crown by length.
  • The shielded loop solves a different problem: its electrostatic shield rejects locally generated electric-field noise, receiving the magnetic component cleanly, which makes it the small-lot and portable listening antenna of choice.
  • Radio direction finding assembles three courses of pieces into a discipline. The small loop's sharp nulls broadside (from General) do the pointing, but a loop alone reads two bearings 180 degrees apart; adding a sense antenna resolves the ambiguity to a single direction. Then triangulation: bearings taken from two or more locations, crossed on a map, yield the transmitter. That is the machinery inside the Technician course's foxhunt and the Volunteer Monitors' beam-heading comparisons from General, now specified well enough to build.

Exam Anchor (E9H) Low-band receive: atmospheric noise dominates, so antenna efficiency matters little and directivity matters greatly. Beverage: one or more wavelengths long for directivity. Shielded loop: rejects local electric-field noise. RDF: loop nulls point, a sense antenna resolves the 180-degree ambiguity, and triangulation from multiple bearings locates the source.
