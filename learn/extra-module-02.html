2.1The Moon, the Meteors, and the Wave Itself

Begin with the wave, stated with Extra precision. An electromagnetic wave's electric and magnetic fields are oriented at right angles to each other, and the wave travels at a right angle to both fields. Its speed through any medium is set by the medium's index of refraction, which is why "the speed of light" always carries the silent asterisk "in vacuum." And a wave whose electric and magnetic fields rotate as it travels is circularly polarized, a definition that becomes an operating tool two paragraphs from now.

EME, Earth-Moon-Earth, is the longest path in amateur radio: bounce a signal off the Moon and the maximum station separation is about 12,000 miles, any two points that can both see the Moon. The path is brutal, and its physics sets the operating practices:

  • Path loss is least when the Moon is at perigee, its closest approach. Serious EME operators schedule by the lunar distance table.
  • The returning echo carries libration fading: a fluttery, irregular fading imposed by the Moon's slight rocking, which smears reflections from its rough surface.
  • The trip through the ionosphere adds Faraday rotation, twisting polarization unpredictably, and satellites add spin modulation. One antenna choice addresses both: circular polarization, which shrugs at rotation because it is already rotating.

Meteor scatter trades the Moon for ionized debris: a meteor burning up creates a linear ionized region in the E region of the ionosphere, and signals bounce off the trail for the seconds it survives. The frequency sweet spot is 28 to 148 MHz, which is why 6 meters is the meteor band and why the Technician course flagged it years ago.

The rest of the specialized menu:

  • Auroral propagation is most likely during severe geomagnetic storms, and the best mode through the shimmer remains CW, the auroral distortion from your Technician course now traced to its solar cause.
  • Microwave tropospheric ducts form most often over large bodies of water, with a typical range of 100 to 300 miles, the General course's duct with its geography and mileage attached.
  • And the daily ionospheric rhythm, stated as an operating decision: when darkness drops the MUF below your long-distance path, switch to a lower frequency band. Follow the MUF down at sunset, back up at sunrise.

2.2Paths the Textbook Diagrams Leave Out

The great-circle hop from your General course is the standard model. This section is everything that breaks it, profitably.

Transequatorial propagation (TEP) links stations 2,000 to 3,000 miles apart on a path perpendicular to the geomagnetic equator, with a maximum range around 5,000 miles, and it favors the afternoon and early evening. Ionization bulges straddling the magnetic equator form a double mirror, and stations in the southern US work South America on bands that should be closed. The magnetic equator, note, not the geographic one; propagation follows the field.

Chordal hop is the ionosphere skipping the middleman: successive ionospheric refractions without an intermediate ground reflection, the signal ricocheting along the underside of the ionosphere. Its effect is the payoff: less loss than multi-hop propagation, because every ground reflection the signal skips is loss it never pays. The freakishly loud long-distance signal at sunset is often riding a chord.

The ionosphere also splits every wave it touches into two: the ordinary and extraordinary waves, independently propagating, elliptically polarized waves created in the ionosphere by the interaction with Earth's magnetic field. Two waves, two slightly different paths, and their interference is part of why HF fading is never simple.

The workhorse facts of the group:

  • Long-path propagation is most frequent on 40 and 20 meters, the bands with the reach to go the wrong way around profitably.
  • 160 meters goes long-distance only on a path entirely in darkness, the D-region absorption story from General taken to its extreme: the top band's DX window is the mutual night.
  • Lowering your takeoff angle increases the distance covered by each hop, the single sentence justifying every antenna height decision you will make in Module 7.
  • Sporadic E gets its calendar and clock: most likely around the solstices, especially the summer solstice, and most likely between sunrise and sunset. June at midday is the Magic Band's rush hour.
  • Ground wave rounds out the map: its range decreases as frequency increases (160 meters crawls a hundred miles; 10 meters barely leaves the yard), and it supports vertical polarization, which is why AM broadcast towers and 160-meter verticals stand up straight.

Exam Anchor (E3B) TEP: 2,000 to 3,000 miles perpendicular to the geomagnetic equator, about 5,000 miles maximum, afternoon and early evening. Chordal hop: successive ionospheric refractions with no ground reflection, hence less loss than multi-hop. Ordinary and extraordinary waves: independent, elliptically polarized waves created in the ionosphere. Long path: most frequent on 40 and 20 meters. 160-meter DX: paths entirely in darkness. Lower takeoff angle: longer hops. Sporadic E: solstices (especially summer), sunrise to sunset. Ground wave: range decreases with frequency; vertical polarization.

2.3Reading Space Weather Like an Engineer

The General course gave you the dashboard. The Extra course gives you the telemetry behind it.

The Sun's misbehavior, classified:

  • Short-term radio blackouts are caused by solar flares, the 8-minute X-ray strike from your General course, now named as the blackout mechanism.
  • Flare intensity runs a letter scale where class X indicates the greatest intensity. An X-flare headline means the low bands are about to go quiet on the daylight side.
  • Geomagnetic storms run the G scale, where G5 is the space-weather term for an extreme storm.
  • A sudden rise in background noise across a large portion of the HF spectrum signals that a coronal mass ejection impact or solar flare has occurred. The bands tell you before the websites do.

The interplanetary detail that predicts trouble: Bz is the north-south component of the interplanetary magnetic field carried by the solar wind. When Bz points southward, it opposes Earth's field, the two fields connect, and charged particles pour in: southward Bz is the loaded gun of geomagnetic disturbance. Watching Bz turn negative during a CME arrival is watching the storm decide to happen.

The indices, upgraded from General: a rising A-index or K-index indicates increasing disturbance of the geomagnetic field, and the paths that suffer most when they climb are those passing through the auroral oval, the high-latitude ring where the particles rain in. Polar paths die first; TEP, meanwhile, barely notices.

The modern instruments:

  • The 304A parameter measures solar UV emissions at 304 angstroms, which correlate with the solar flux index: a direct optical measurement of the ionizing radiation itself.
  • VOACAP software models HF propagation, the free planning tool that turns all of this telemetry into a point-to-point reliability forecast.
  • And amateur propagation reporting networks (the Reverse Beacon Network and PSK Reporter from your General capstone) report digital-mode and CW signals, real-time confirmation that beats any model.

One last geometry fact files the radio horizon precisely: the VHF/UHF radio horizon is approximately 15 percent farther than the geographic horizon, the Technician course's "slightly past the visible horizon" finally assigned its number.

Before you move on
