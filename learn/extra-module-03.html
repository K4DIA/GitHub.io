Extra Course, Module 3: The Mathematics of Resonance

Covers NCVEC 2024-2028 Extra Pool, Subelement E5 (Electrical Principles). Four questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray writes one formula across the top of the whiteboard and boxes it: f = 1 / (2π√LC). "Every course so far treated resonance as a fact. This one treats it as arithmetic. Four sections: resonance with numbers, time and phase, the coordinate systems that tame complex impedance, and what RF does to real parts. This is the most math in the whole program, and every line of it drives something you own."

3.1Resonance, Quantified

The boxed formula computes the resonant frequency of any LC pair. The pool's two:

  • 50 µH and 40 pF: 3.56 MHz.
  • 50 µH and 10 pF: 7.12 MHz.

Notice the check: one quarter the capacitance doubled the frequency, because C sits under a square root. That relationship is worth more than the formula.

At resonance, the reactances cancel (your General definition), and what remains is telling:

  • A series RLC circuit's impedance at resonance is approximately equal to the circuit resistance: the reactances vanish from the equation and R is what is left. Voltage and current are in phase.
  • A parallel RLC circuit's impedance at resonance is also approximately equal to the circuit resistance, but the geometry inverts everything else: the circulating current between L and C is at maximum while the current at the circuit's input is at minimum. The tank sloshes furiously inside while sipping from the source.

Q, the quality factor, measures how furiously. For a parallel resonant circuit, Q = resistance divided by reactance. Its two consequences run in opposite directions, and both are exam material:

  • Bandwidth. The half-power bandwidth of a resonant circuit is f / Q. A 7.1 MHz circuit with Q of 150: 47.3 kHz. A 3.7 MHz circuit with Q of 118: 31.4 kHz. High Q buys selectivity by narrowing the window, which is also why increasing the Q of an impedance-matching circuit decreases its matching bandwidth, the reason your mobile antenna from the General course needs retuning across one band.
  • Voltage magnification. Resonance can drive the voltage across the reactances higher than the voltage applied to the whole circuit, and increasing Q in a series resonant circuit increases those internal voltages further. A modest transmitter can develop startling kilovolts across a high-Q loop capacitor. Q is free voltage gain, and free voltage gain bites.

Exam Anchor (E5A) f = 1/(2π√LC): 50 µH + 40 pF = 3.56 MHz; 50 µH + 10 pF = 7.12 MHz. Series resonance: impedance ≈ R, voltage and current in phase; resonance can raise reactance voltages above the applied voltage, and higher Q raises them further. Parallel resonance: impedance ≈ R, circulating current maximum, input current minimum. Parallel Q = R / X. Half-power bandwidth = f/Q: 47.3 kHz at 7.1 MHz with Q 150; 31.4 kHz at 3.7 MHz with Q 118. Higher matching-circuit Q: narrower matching bandwidth.

3.2Time Constants and the Phase Dance

Two ideas govern circuits in motion: how fast they settle, and who leads whom.

The time constant measures settling: one time constant is the time for a capacitor in an RC circuit to charge to 63.2 percent of the applied voltage, or discharge to 36.8 percent of its initial voltage. The pool's computation: two 220 µF capacitors and two 1-megohm resistors, all in parallel. Module 3 of the General course does the combining (parallel capacitors add: 440 µF; parallel resistors halve: 0.5 megohm), and τ = RC = 220 seconds. That nearly-four-minute answer is also a safety lesson: big filter capacitors bleed slowly, which is why the bleeder resistor exists and why "off" is not "safe."

The phase dance starts with two partners you can memorize with one phrase, ELI the ICE man:

  • In an inductor (L), voltage (E) leads current (I): ELI.
  • In a capacitor (C), current (I) leads voltage (E): ICE.
  • Both by 90 degrees in the pure component.

Mix R with net reactance and the angle lands between 0 and 90: the tangent of the phase angle is net reactance over resistance. The pool's three, each with the same recipe (net the reactances, divide by R, arctangent):

  • XC 500, R 1000, XL 250: net 250 capacitive, angle 14 degrees, voltage lagging (capacitive circuits lag, per ICE).
  • XC 300, R 100, XL 100: net 200 capacitive, angle 63 degrees, voltage lagging.
  • XC 25, R 100, XL 75: net 50 inductive, angle 27 degrees, voltage leading (ELI).

The reciprocal world completes your vocabulary: admittance is the inverse of impedance (from General), and its imaginary part is susceptance, represented by the letter B. Converting impedance in polar form to admittance: take the reciprocal of the magnitude and change the sign of the angle. Converting a pure reactance to susceptance: replace it with its reciprocal. Parallel-circuit analysis runs on admittances the way series analysis runs on impedances, which is the entire reason this mirror-image vocabulary exists.

Exam Anchor (E5B) One time constant: charge to 63.2 percent or discharge to 36.8 percent. Two 220 µF caps and two 1 MΩ resistors all in parallel: 220 seconds. Capacitor: current leads voltage by 90 degrees; inductor: voltage leads current by 90 degrees. Phase angles: 14 degrees lagging (XC 500, R 1000, XL 250); 63 degrees lagging (XC 300, R 100, XL 100); 27 degrees leading (XC 25, R 100, XL 75). Admittance: inverse of impedance; susceptance: its imaginary part, letter B; polar conversion: reciprocal magnitude, negated angle; reactance to susceptance: reciprocal.

3.3Complex Impedance on Paper

Impedance has two dimensions, so plotting it takes two axes, and two coordinate systems share the job.

Rectangular coordinates split impedance into parts: the X axis carries the resistive component, the Y axis the reactive component, written as R ± jX. The conventions:

  • Pure resistance sits on the horizontal axis.
  • Inductive reactance is +j (up); capacitive is -j (down). A pure capacitive reactance of 100 ohms is 0 - j100.
  • 50 - j25 reads as 50 ohms of resistance in series with 25 ohms of capacitive reactance. The minus sign is the whole diagnosis.

Polar coordinates describe the same point by magnitude and phase angle, which is how your antenna analyzer thinks. Pure inductive reactance in polar form is a positive 90-degree angle; pure capacitive, negative 90. A phasor diagram is the drawing that shows phase relationships between impedances at a given frequency, Section 3.2's dance rendered as arrows.

The pool exercises this on Figure E5-1, a rectangular grid of numbered points where you compute a reactance (X = 1/2πfC or 2πfL) and pick the point at (R, ±jX). The three worked cases: 400 ohms with 38 pF at 14 MHz lands near 400 - j300; 300 ohms with 18 µH at 3.505 MHz near 300 + j400; 300 ohms with 19 pF at 21.2 MHz near 300 - j400. Compute the reactance, mind the sign, find the point.

Website note: this page will include a redrawn E5-1 grid with worked solutions traced onto it, plus a blank version for practice.

One graphing convention finishes the section: frequency-response curves use a logarithmic Y-axis scale, because filter responses spanning 60 dB are unreadable on linear paper. Every filter plot you have seen since the General course was quietly logarithmic; now it is official.

3.4What RF Does to Real Parts

The General course warned that at RF every part is all three of R, L, and C. This section names the mechanisms.

Skin effect is the big one: as frequency increases, RF current flows closer to the conductor's surface, so resistance increases with frequency because the current uses less of the copper. Consequences ripple everywhere: it is the primary loss mechanism in film capacitors at RF, it is why RF conductors are silver-plated and why the General course preferred wide strap, and it adds a subtlety to antennas: as a conductor's diameter increases, its electrical length increases, which is why fat elements resonate slightly lower than the wire formula predicts (and, from General Module 7, why they are broader banded).

Parasitics are the components you did not install:

  • Every component's self-resonance comes from its nominal reactance combining with its parasitic reactance. In an inductor specifically, the parasite is inter-turn capacitance, the General course's self-resonant coil now explained.
  • Electrolytic capacitors are unsuitable at RF because of their parasitic inductance, that rolled-foil construction being effectively a coil.
  • The cures are geometric: keep component leads short at VHF and above to minimize inductive reactance, and at microwave, short connections reduce phase shift along the connection, because at 10 GHz a centimeter of wire is an appreciable fraction of a wavelength.

Real and reactive power close the module. In reactive power, voltage and current are 90 degrees out of phase, and the result is genuinely strange: ideal inductors and capacitors store energy in their fields but dissipate no power. Reactive power is wattless, nonproductive power, volt-amperes circulating without heating anything. Only the resistive component consumes: a circuit of 100 ohms resistance in series with 100 ohms inductive reactance drawing 1 ampere consumes I²R = 100 watts of real power, not the 141 the total impedance suggests. Your wattmeter, your power bill, and your finals all agree: only R gets paid.

Exam Anchor (E5D) Skin effect: RF current crowds the surface, resistance rises with frequency; it is the main film-capacitor RF loss; larger conductor diameter increases electrical length. Self-resonance: nominal plus parasitic reactance; in inductors, inter-turn capacitance. Electrolytics fail at RF from parasitic inductance. Short leads at VHF: minimize inductive reactance; short microwave connections: reduce phase shift. Reactive power: voltage and current 90 degrees apart; ideal L and C store energy without dissipation; reactive power is wattless and nonproductive. 100 ohms R in series with 100 ohms XL at 1 A: 100 watts real power.

Answer Resistance increases as frequency increases because RF current flows closer to the surface

E5D02 Why is it important to keep lead lengths short for components used in circuits for VHF and above?

Answer To minimize inductive reactance

Tip Every bit of wire adds inductive reactance, so at VHF and up you keep leads short to hold that reactance down.

E5D03 What is the phase relationship between current and voltage for reactive power?

Answer They are 90 degrees out of phase

Tip Reactive power flows when current and voltage are 90 degrees out of phase, so no net energy is delivered.

E5D04 Why are short connections used at microwave frequencies?

Answer To reduce phase shift along the connection

Tip Electrolytics have rolled construction that adds series inductance, which spoils them for RF use.

E5D06 What parasitic characteristic creates an inductor’s self-resonance?

Answer Inter-turn capacitance

E5D07 What combines to create the self-resonance of a component?

Answer The component’s nominal and parasitic reactance

E5D08 What is the primary cause of loss in film capacitors at RF?

Answer Skin effect

E5D09 What happens to reactive power in ideal inductors and capacitors?

Answer Energy is stored in magnetic or electric fields, but power is not dissipated

E5D10 As a conductor’s diameter increases, what is the effect on its electrical length?

Answer Wattless, nonproductive power
