Extra Course, Module 6: Signals in Full

Covers NCVEC 2024-2028 Extra Pool, Subelement E8 (Signals and Emissions). Four questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray keys a dead-plain carrier into the dummy load and puts the spectrum display beside the oscilloscope. "One line on the analyzer, one sine wave on the scope. Same signal, two true descriptions. Everything in this module is learning to move between those two views, because every signal you will ever make or measure lives in both."

6.1Fourier's Great Idea, and the Honest Digitizer

Fourier analysis is the mathematical bridge between Ray's two screens: any repetitive waveform can be expressed as a sum of sine waves, a fundamental plus harmonics in the right amounts and phases. Two canonical decompositions the exam checks:

  • A square wave is the fundamental plus its odd harmonics (3rd, 5th, 7th...), which is why Section 5.2's switching amplifiers need harmonic filters and why key clicks splatter: sharp edges are high harmonics.
  • A sawtooth wave contains the fundamental plus all harmonics, odd and even.

Change the time-domain shape and you have changed the spectrum, and vice versa; they are one object.

The measurement vocabulary carries forward with its reasons: RMS matters because it states a waveform's equivalent heating power (the General definition, now the answer to "why measure RMS at all"), and PEP-versus-average keeps its General-course arithmetic, with one addition worth knowing for real signals: typical SSB voice runs a PEP-to-average ratio around 2.5 to 1, which is why your wattmeter's average needle sits so far below the peaks.

Digitizing a signal honestly obeys Section 5.6's law: sample at twice the highest frequency or faster. One counterintuitive refinement joins it: dither, a small amount of noise deliberately added before analog-to-digital conversion, which breaks up quantization artifacts and improves the converter's effective linearity. Adding noise to increase fidelity is the kind of trick that separates Extra-class intuition from arithmetic: quantization error is correlated with the signal and sounds like distortion; dither randomizes it into benign hiss.

Exam Anchor (E8A) Fourier: any repetitive waveform is a sum of sine waves. Square wave: fundamental plus odd harmonics. Sawtooth: fundamental plus all harmonics. RMS: the equivalent heating power measure. SSB voice PEP-to-average: roughly 2.5:1. Sampling: at least twice the highest frequency. Dither: small added noise that improves ADC linearity by randomizing quantization error.

6.2Modulation by the Numbers, and Sharing the Channel

FM finally gets its quantities:

  • Modulation index is the ratio of frequency deviation to the modulating frequency, computed instant by instant: 6 kHz of deviation from a 2 kHz tone is an index of 3. Note what it ignores: modulation index does not depend on the RF carrier frequency, only on deviation and audio.
  • Deviation ratio is the design-limit version: maximum deviation divided by maximum modulating frequency. The pool's pair: 5 kHz deviation with 3 kHz maximum audio gives 1.67; 7.5 kHz with 3.5 kHz gives 2.14.

These numbers connect straight back to the General course's E1C modulation-index limit of 1.0 below 29 MHz and to Carson's-rule bandwidth: the index is the dial that trades bandwidth for noise immunity.

Sharing one channel among many signals is multiplexing, in three geometries:

  • Frequency-division multiplexing (FDM): two or more information streams ride different frequencies simultaneously.
  • Time-division multiplexing (TDM): streams take turns in time slots on one frequency, and you have operated it since the Technician course: DMR's two voices per channel is TDM wearing a callsign.
  • Orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM): many closely spaced subcarriers, mathematically orthogonal so they interleave without interfering, each carrying a slow piece of a fast data stream. Its superpower is resistance to multipath and intersymbol interference: slow symbols on many carriers survive the smearing that would destroy fast symbols on one. OFDM is inside digital television, mesh networking, and the wideband digital voice modes, and it is the reason those systems shrug at reflections that would wreck an analog picture.

Exam Anchor (E8B) Modulation index: deviation divided by modulating frequency (6 kHz over 2 kHz = 3), independent of carrier frequency. Deviation ratio: maximum deviation over maximum modulating frequency (5/3 = 1.67; 7.5/3.5 = 2.14). FDM: streams on different frequencies. TDM: streams in time slots. OFDM: many orthogonal subcarriers; resists multipath and intersymbol interference.

6.3Digital Signals and Their Geometry

The digital vocabulary, made precise:

  • Symbol rate is the rate at which the transmitted waveform changes state, and Module 6 of the General course already taught its law: higher symbol rates require wider bandwidth. Bits and symbols part company when each state change carries multiple bits, which is the entire trick of dense modern modes.
  • The bandwidth arithmetic the pool checks: a 13-WPM CW signal occupies roughly 52.8 Hz (bandwidth scales with keying speed, about 4 Hz per WPM); a 170-Hz-shift, 300-baud ASCII signal needs about 0.5 kHz; a 4800-Hz-shift, 9600-baud FSK signal needs about 15.36 kHz. Different modes, one principle: bandwidth follows shift plus speed.
  • Gray code orders binary values so that only one bit changes between successive values. Its payoff in modulation: a symbol mistaken for its nearest neighbor corrupts only one bit, converting the likeliest channel error into the smallest data error.

The geometry: a constellation diagram plots a digitally modulated signal's states as points, with phase as the angle and amplitude as the distance from the origin. QPSK draws four points on a circle (phase only, the four shifts from the General course); QAM populates a grid, encoding in both amplitude and phase at once, buying bits per symbol at the price of noise margin. Read a constellation like a target: tight clusters mean a clean channel, smeared clusters mean noise, rotated clusters mean phase trouble. One diagram, a complete diagnosis.

Error protection completes the picture with the General course's FEC generalized: redundancy spent up front (as in FT8's deep coding) buys decoding below the noise, and the harder the channel, the better that trade gets.

Exam Anchor (E8C) Symbol rate: how fast the waveform changes state. Bandwidths: 13-WPM CW about 52.8 Hz; 170-Hz shift 300-baud ASCII about 0.5 kHz; 4800-Hz shift 9600-baud about 15.36 kHz. Gray code: one bit changes between successive values, minimizing the damage of nearest-neighbor symbol errors. Constellation diagram: phase as angle, amplitude as radius. QAM: amplitude and phase together.

6.4Clean Keying, Codes, and Spread Spectrum

The transmitter-hygiene section, Fourier's idea enforced:

  • Key clicks are caused by excessively fast CW rise and fall times: sharp edges are odd harmonics (Section 6.1), and they land on your neighbors. The cure is shaping the keying envelope to a rise and fall of about 5 milliseconds, slow enough to tame the sidebands, fast enough to keep crisp code.
  • Overdriven digital modes commit the same sin the analog way: the General course's waterfall sidebars, now understood as intermodulation products of an overdriven audio chain. Digital cleanliness is Module 5's amplifier linearity plus this module's spectral awareness, in daily practice.

Character codes, compared:

  • Baudot remains the 5-bit RTTY code from the General course. ASCII is the 7-bit code, and its advantages are practical: upper and lower case, and an optional parity bit enabling simple error detection. One bit of redundancy catches any single-bit error, the cheapest error detection ever invented, and the conceptual seed of everything FEC.

Spread spectrum closes the signals course, the mode Extra rules finally let you transmit (above 222 MHz, 10 watts, from Module 1):

  • Direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) multiplies the data by a fast pseudorandom sequence, smearing a narrow signal across a wide band at low density.
  • Frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) changes the carrier frequency per a pseudorandom sequence shared by both stations.
  • The interference resistance of both comes from the same lock-and-key mechanism: the receiver, synchronized to the pseudorandom sequence, despreads the desired signal while signals not synchronized to the sequence are ignored or diluted. To everyone else, your signal is a whisper of noise; to your partner, it assembles perfectly. It is the one legal amateur mode that hides in plain sight, which is precisely why the rules fence its power and frequencies.

Exam Anchor (E8D) Key clicks: too-fast rise and fall; shape to about 5 ms. Baudot: 5 bits; ASCII: 7 bits, upper and lower case, optional parity bit for error detection. DSSS: data multiplied by a fast pseudorandom sequence. FHSS: carrier hops per a pseudorandom sequence. Spread spectrum interference resistance: unsynchronized signals are ignored by the despreading receiver.
