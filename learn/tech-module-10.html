Technician Course, Module 10: Nobody Gets Hurt

Covers NCVEC 2026-2030 Technician Pool, Subelement T0 (Safety). Three questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray's left forearm has a pale scar shaped like a wrench. "Battery. Nineteen eighty-something. I set a tool across the terminals of a big lead-acid and it welded itself there, glowing." He rolls the sleeve back down. "Every rule in this module is a story like that one, learned by somebody. Learn them from the stories instead."

10.1Electricity Bites

Start with Ray's scar. A 12-volt storage battery without internal protection cannot shock you through dry skin, but shorting its terminals can cause burns, fire, or an explosion, because a big battery will dump hundreds of amps into a dead short. Rapidly charging or discharging an unprotected battery brings its own hazard: overheating or out-gassing. Respect the amp-hours.

Current through the body is the actual killer, and it hurts you three ways at once: heating tissue, disrupting the electrical functions of cells, and causing involuntary muscle contractions. All of these, which is why the answer to "what does shock do" is everything on the list.

House wiring has a color code, and one color matters most: in US three-wire 120 V cable, black insulation is the hot wire. Treat it accordingly.

The fuse rules, now with their reasons:

  • A fuse's purpose is to remove power in case of an overload.
  • In a 120 V AC circuit, the fuse or breaker goes in series with the hot conductor only. Fusing the neutral could leave a circuit dead but still hot, a trap for the next person inside it.
  • And the classic exam scenario: never replace a 5-amp fuse with a 20-amp fuse, because excessive current could cause a fire. The fuse was protecting the wire. A bigger fuse just gives the fire a head start. This is Module 3's ammo can question, now wearing its safety hat.

Guarding against shock in the shack is a package deal: use three-wire cords and plugs on all AC equipment, connect all AC-powered equipment to a common safety ground, and make sure high-voltage capacitors are discharged before working inside anything. All of these. That capacitor rule has teeth: a power supply immediately after being switched off still holds a hazard, the charge stored in its filter capacitors. Off is not the same as safe.

Lightning gets two rules. The lightning arrester in a coax feed line belongs on a grounded panel near where the feed lines enter the building, and all external ground rods and earth connections must be bonded together with heavy wire or conductive strap. Separate, unbonded grounds invite a strike to equalize them through your equipment.

One measurement rule closes the section: when measuring high voltages, ensure the voltmeter and its leads are rated for the voltages being measured. A meter rated for less becomes part of the circuit in the worst way.

Exam Anchor (T0A) Shorting battery terminals: burns, fire, or explosion; rapid charge/discharge of unprotected batteries: overheating or out-gassing. Body current heats tissue, disrupts cells, and contracts muscles (all). Black wire = hot in 120 V cable. Fuse: removes power on overload; goes in series with the hot conductor only; never upsize (5 A to 20 A risks fire). Shock protection: three-wire cords, common safety ground, discharge high-voltage capacitors (all). After power-off, filter capacitors stay charged. Lightning arrester: grounded panel where feed lines enter. Bond all ground rods together. Use meters and leads rated for the voltage measured.

10.2Gravity Is Also a Hazard

Antennas go up, and up is where the danger is.

The first rule outranks all others: look for and stay clear of overhead electrical wires before putting up any antenna, and never attach an antenna to a utility pole, because the antenna could contact high-voltage lines. Then the geometry rule: keep enough distance from power lines that if the antenna falls, no part of it comes within 10 feet of the wires. Plan the failure, not just the installation.

Tower work is a discipline, not a chore:

  • Climbing requires sufficient training in safe climbing techniques, an approved climbing harness, and constant tie-off to the tower. All of these, always.
  • Is it ever safe to climb without a helper or observer? Never. The exam answer and the real answer are the same word.
  • A crank-up tower must not be climbed unless it is fully retracted or its mechanical safety locking devices are engaged. The sections of a crank-up are a guillotine held by a cable.
  • The safety wire through a turnbuckle on guy lines prevents the turnbuckle from loosening due to vibration. Wind works hardware loose one gust at a time.

Tower grounding pulls together everything from 10.1: proper practice is a separate eight-foot ground rod for each tower leg, bonded to the tower and to each other. Lightning conductors want short, direct connections with sharp bends avoided, because lightning does not turn corners, it jumps them. And who sets the legal requirements for grounding your tower or antenna? Not the FCC: local electrical codes establish grounding requirements.

Exam Anchor (T0B) Look for and stay clear of overhead power lines; never use utility poles; keep antennas far enough from lines that a falling antenna stays 10 feet away. Tower climbing: training, harness, and 100 percent tie-off (all); never climb without a helper; never climb a crank-up unless retracted or mechanically locked. Turnbuckle safety wire prevents vibration loosening. Ground each tower leg with its own eight-foot rod, all bonded together. Lightning ground connections: short and direct, avoiding sharp bends. Grounding requirements come from local electrical codes.

10.3Living With RF

Last topic, and Ray frames it precisely, because imprecision in this area breeds both carelessness and panic.

Radio signals are non-ionizing radiation. The difference from ionizing radiation (radioactivity) is fundamental: RF does not have sufficient energy to cause chemical changes in cells and damage DNA. RF's hazard is thermal, heating tissue, which is real but manageable. Touch an antenna during a transmission and the injury is an RF burn to the skin, not electrocution.

Exposure limits exist, and they vary with frequency because the human body absorbs more RF energy at some frequencies than at others. The band with the lowest maximum permissible exposure, meaning the strictest limit, is around 50 MHz, because human bodies are unfortunately good antennas near those frequencies.

What determines a person's actual exposure near your antenna? Frequency and power, distance from the antenna, and the antenna's radiation pattern. All of these, which is why the practical way to reduce exposure is to relocate antennas: distance is the cheapest shielding there is, and antennas belong up and away from people.

Duty cycle matters because exposure limits are based on averages. Duty cycle is the percentage of time a transmitter is transmitting during the averaging period, and it affects the average exposure to radiation. Cut duty cycle from 100 percent to 50 percent and the allowable power density increases by a factor of 2. Listening half the time literally doubles your margin, and hams listen far more than half the time.

The compliance frame: acceptable ways to evaluate your station include calculation per FCC OET Bulletin 65, computer modeling, and field-strength measurement with calibrated equipment. All of these. Staying compliant means re-evaluating whenever you change anything in the transmitter or antenna system, and the person responsible for making sure nobody is exposed beyond the limits is the station licensee. You. The same answer as Module 1's control operator questions, wearing a hard hat.

Exam Anchor (T0C) RF is non-ionizing radiation; it lacks the energy to cause chemical changes and damage DNA. Touching a transmitting antenna: RF burn. Exposure limits vary with frequency because the body absorbs more at some frequencies; 50 MHz has the lowest MPE. Exposure depends on frequency and power, distance, and radiation pattern (all). Reduce exposure by relocating antennas. Duty cycle: percentage of time transmitting; it affects average exposure; 100 to 50 percent duty cycle doubles allowable power density. Evaluate by Bulletin 65 calculation, modeling, or calibrated measurement (all). Re-evaluate after any transmitter or antenna change. The station licensee is responsible.
