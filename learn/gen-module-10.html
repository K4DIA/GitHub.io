General Course, Module 10: Safety at HF Power

Covers NCVEC 2023-2027 General Pool, Subelement G0 (Electrical and RF Safety). Two questions on your exam come from this material.

Ray walks the whole station one last time, hand trailing along the bonding strap. "Technician safety was about not getting hurt by a radio," he says. "General safety is about running a hundred watts, or fifteen hundred, next to people you love, forever, with total confidence that nobody is being cooked, shocked, or surprised. It is the same rules with bigger numbers, so we finish where we started: nobody gets hurt."

10.1RF Exposure, Now That You Have Real Power

The Technician foundation stands: RF is non-ionizing, and its effect on the body is heating tissue. What changes at General is that your power and your antennas got serious, so the evaluation becomes personal engineering instead of a checkbox.

Whether a signal exceeds the maximum permissible exposure (MPE) limits depends on its frequency, its power density, and its duty cycle. All of these, the same three-legged stool from Technician, now supporting 100-watt legs. The evaluation methods also carry over intact: calculation per FCC OET Bulletin 65, computer modeling, or measurement with calibrated field-strength equipment. All acceptable.

The concepts that do the heavy lifting at HF power:

  • Duty cycle remains the percentage of time transmitting during the averaging period, and its practical consequence is stated plainly: a lower duty cycle permits greater power levels for the same average exposure. A CW conversation at 50 percent duty and low key-down density exposes far less than the same power of key-down FT8, which is Module 6's finals-cooking warning wearing its safety hat.
  • Time averaging is the principle underneath: RF exposure is averaged over time, because the hazard is cumulative heating, not instantaneous fields. Bodies, like transistors, care about average power.

The obligations, and they are yours:

  • Perform the routine station evaluation to determine whether your station complies with MPE limits, and repeat it when anything in the transmitter or antenna system changes.
  • If the evaluation shows exposure limits exceeded where people can be, take action to prevent human exposure: relocate the antenna, reduce power, restrict access to the area, or fence the time people spend there. Two specific versions the exam names: if a directional antenna's main lobe would exceed limits at a neighbor's location, ensure the antenna cannot be pointed in their direction; and an indoor transmitting antenna demands verifying that MPE limits are not exceeded in occupied areas, because indoors, everywhere is the near field. The fix is required, not suggested.
  • Before working on an antenna, make sure transmitters that feed it cannot transmit: powered down, disconnected, and everyone in the household briefed. An antenna under maintenance is an antenna off the air, no exceptions, which also finishes the RF burn story from Technician: the burn happens when the antenna and the hand meet during transmission, so the rule ensures they never can.

Exam Anchor (G0A) RF affects the body by heating tissue. MPE depends on frequency, power density, and duty cycle (all). Evaluate by Bulletin 65 calculation, modeling, or calibrated measurement (all). Lower duty cycle permits higher power for the same exposure; exposure is time-averaged. Re-evaluate after station changes. If limits are exceeded, take action to prevent human exposure: relocating antennas is the named remedy, a directional antenna must not be pointable at an over-limit neighbor location, and indoor antennas require MPE compliance in occupied areas. Antenna work: turn off the transmitter and disconnect the feed line. Touching a transmitting antenna: RF burn.

10.2Wiring, Generators, and the Tower, at Full Scale

The electrical rules scale up from Technician with numbers attached.

Wiring by the book:

  • Fuses and circuit breakers go in series with the hot conductor only, the Technician rule unchanged and worth repeating forever.
  • Wire gauge now has values: AWG 12 for a 20-ampere circuit, AWG 10 for 30 amperes, and the rule runs both directions: AWG 14 wire must be protected by a fuse or breaker of no more than 15 amperes. Wire is rated by what warms it, and the fuse from Technician Module 10 is still protecting the wire, not the radio.
  • Inside equipment, two capacitor disciplines: bleeder resistors (Module 5) discharge supply capacitors at power-off, and before working inside high-voltage equipment you verify the discharge yourself with a meter rated for the job. Interlocks, whose purpose is to remove dangerous voltages when the cabinet is opened, are respected, never defeated.
  • One bench-habit rule the exam likes: after soldering, wash your hands. Lead-tin solder is a skin-contact and hand-to-mouth hazard, and the cure costs thirty seconds of soap.

Generators power the emergency station and bring two killers to manage. The first is carbon monoxide: a generator runs outdoors, well away from building openings, windows, doors, and vents, full stop. The second is invisible from your side of the meter: when a generator powers your house, you must disconnect the incoming utility power feed first. Backfeeding the grid can energize "dead" lines and kill the lineworker restoring your neighborhood, which makes the transfer switch the most important safety device in your emergency power plan.

The tower repeats the Technician discipline with the crew formalized:

  • Climbing gear, harness and lanyards, is inspected before each use, and the climber maintains 100 percent tie-off at all times, with tools raised on a line rather than carried in hand.
  • The ground crew is part of the safety system: anyone standing below wears a hard hat and safety glasses the entire time someone is aloft. Dropped wrenches obey Module 10 of the Technician course: gravity is also a hazard.
  • Grounding at full scale: each tower leg gets its own ground rod, all bonded together, with lightning conductors run short and direct, avoiding sharp bends (lightning, all rise-time and no patience, treats a sharp bend as an inductor and jumps it), and lightning arrestors on a grounded panel where the feed lines enter the building. Every element of that sentence appeared somewhere earlier in your training; here it assembles into one drawing, which is exactly what your capstone's station-design documentation asks you to produce.

Exam Anchor (G0B) Fuses and breakers: hot conductor only. AWG 12 for 20 A; AWG 10 for 30 A; AWG 14 fused at no more than 15 A. Discharge and verify capacitors before internal work; respect interlocks. Wash hands after handling lead solder. Generators: outdoors, away from building openings, because of carbon monoxide; disconnect the incoming utility feed before powering the house. Climbing gear inspected before each use; 100 percent tie-off; ground crew in hard hats and safety glasses. Tower grounding: a bonded rod per leg, short direct conductors avoiding sharp bends, arrestors on a grounded entry panel.
