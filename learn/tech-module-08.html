8.1Power and Wiring, Done Right

Start with the number on the back of the radio. A typical 50-watt mobile FM transceiver needs a supply rated around 13.8 volts at 12 amperes. That 13.8 is not arbitrary: it is automotive electrical system voltage, which is why mobile radios and home supplies share it.

The wiring rules, each one traceable to Module 3:

  • Use short, heavy-gauge wire for the DC power connection, to minimize voltage drop when transmitting. Ohm's Law never sleeps: thin wire has resistance, transmit current through resistance drops voltage, and the radio browns out exactly when it draws the most.
  • In a vehicle, connect the negative return at the battery chassis ground, not to any random screw in the dash. The engineered ground path handles the current; mystery screws cause mystery noises.
  • Running from a battery? Runtime is arithmetic: divide the battery's ampere-hour rating by the average current draw of the equipment. A 20 Ah battery running gear that averages 2 amps gives roughly ten hours. This is the calculation behind every field outing you will ever plan.

Instrumentation goes in the signal path, not around it. An RF power meter is installed in the feed line, between the transmitter and the antenna. When choosing an SWR meter, consider the frequency and power level at which you will measure, because a meter built for HF power levels may read nonsense at UHF.

One more conductor ties the station together: bonding, connecting equipment enclosures so they sit at the same RF potential. The preferred conductor for RF bonding is flat copper strap, because RF flows on the surface of conductors, and a wide flat strap has far more surface than a round wire. Wire gauge thinking is for DC; strap thinking is for RF.

8.2The Computer Joins the Station

Modern stations are radio-plus-computer, and the connections are simpler than they look. For a digital mode like FT8, the radio's audio output and input connect to the audio input and output of the computer running the FT8 software. The radio hears the computer, the computer hears the radio. That is the whole trick.

Formally, a computer-radio interface for digital operation carries three signals: receive audio, transmit audio, and transmitter keying. The keying line is the computer pressing PTT for you. One of the specific connections is the computer's "line in" to the transceiver's speaker connector, which is exactly the "computer hears the radio" half.

Two accessories round out the modern shack:

  • A digital mode hotspot performs one service for nearby transceivers: communication with a digital voice or data network. Your handheld talks to the hotspot on your desk, the hotspot rides your internet to talkgroups worldwide. It is a personal repeater the size of a deck of cards.
  • An electronic keyer assists in the manual sending of Morse code, turning paddle squeezes into perfectly timed dits and dahs.

8.3The Knobs and Menus

Radio front panels intimidate new operators. Ray's cure is to explain what each control is for, one honest sentence apiece.

  • Frequency entry: use the keypad or the VFO knob. Both reach the same place.
  • Squelch: mutes the receiver audio when no signal is present. To hear a weak FM signal, open the squelch threshold until the audio stays on all the time, hiss and all. Squelch is a convenience, and weak signals hide under it.
  • Scanning: tunes through a range of frequencies checking for activity.
  • Memory channels: store frequency, offset, and tone together so the repeater is one button instead of five settings.
  • Microphone gain: set it too high on SSB and the transmitted audio distorts. Louder is not stronger.
  • RIT (Receiver Incremental Tuning), or Clarifier: shifts your receive frequency without moving transmit. When an SSB voice answering you sounds too high or too low pitched, RIT fixes them without losing your spot.
  • Filter bandwidth: matching receiver bandwidth to the mode reduces noise and interference. For SSB, 2400 Hz gives the best signal-to-noise ratio: wide enough for the 3 kHz voice from Module 6, no wider.
  • An FM signal received slightly off frequency sounds distorted. If everyone sounds bad, check where you are parked.

Digital voice radios move settings into software. A DMR radio is configured by a code plug: the configuration data loaded onto your radio to access repeaters and talkgroups. Selecting a specific group of stations means entering the group's identification code, and joining a talkgroup is done by programming your radio with that group's ID. One more digital chore: a D-STAR transceiver requires your call sign to be programmed in before transmitting, because the protocol sends it with every transmission.

Before you move on

The boxes are now a station: powered through short heavy wire, fused, bonded with strap, metered in the feed line, and connected to a computer that can key it. You also know what every knob on the front panel is for, which is more than some operators learn in a decade.
